### Exercise 1.3:

## List five areas of your life that could be games. Then briefly describe a possible underlying game structure for each.

<Steps>
  <Step title="House Renovations">
    When I first started tracking down a pesky leak inside my wall, I had to diagnose exactly where the water was coming from. I set strict rules and constraints for this task to shape my decision-making: I had to track the leak with minimal drywall damage. I chased the water from the basement, through the main floor, and finally to the space underneath my upstairs bedroom window.
    
    For me, the "name of the game" was efficiency and problem-solving. The goal was to open the wall just enough to dry everything out without creating unnecessary repair work. I see this as having strategic and exploratory game structures, where the player acts as the designer of their own constraints.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Teaching Grade Two">
    Engaging students in literacy or numeracy activities in my Grade 2 class can sometimes be tricky. Recently, I gamified our math lesson by introducing a twist on BINGO; instead of calling out numbers, I called out problems like "2 + 3" for them to solve.
    
    One underlying game structure for BINGO could be the simultaneous rounds and turn taking whereby everyone takes their turn at the exact same time resulting in a bit of action and suspense each round. Additionally, BINGO acts as a platform; once the students know the rules, the game becomes a vehicle for delivering all sorts of different content through a familiar interface.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Playing Jazz Guitar">
    When I practice, I often turn the session into a game by imposing strict constraints on my improvisation to see if I can still make music or play through the chord changes to a song. I might tell myself, "This time, I can only play arpeggios over the changes," or "I must play every dominant seven chord with a sharp five."
    
    The underlying game structure here is the coordination of knowledge and ability around a set task. By creating self-imposed challenges, I am effectively using rule-setting to enhance my creative practice and stretch my knowledge and ability on guitar so that I don't "play myself into a corner" when improvising on the guitar.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Going Grocery Shopping">
    Navigating the grocery store during rush hour often feels like a real-time strategy game. Get the groceries, make it to the cashier and avoid running into people I know is often the task. I find myself constantly crunching numbers to determine if a bulk item is actually a good deal, or racing against the clock to get in and out as fast as possible. 
    
    The game structures at play here involves resource management (time, money, attention) and navigating the unpredictability of other "players" in the environment. It requires a constant situational awareness of oneself in relation to the chaos of the aisles.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Cooking">
    When I am making lunch during my short lunch break, I mean business. The objective is to optimize the cooking process so efficiently that I actually have time left over to sit and enjoy the meal. 
    
    The underlying game structure here is pure self-challenge and optimization. It is a race against a timer where the win-state is a hot meal and a few minutes of peace, and the lose-state is rushing back to work hungry while still eating my scrambled eggs.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Callout variant="info" title="My thoughts">
  <div>
    Through the above, I would say that the trends I found myself thinking about most are the <sparkly-text className="inline-block" number-of-sparkles="5">"playcentric"</sparkly-text> elements often superimposed onto games, including challenges, patterns, opportunities for meaning-making, and the optimization of a process or narrative.
  </div>

The game felt fast-paced, familiar, simple, and addictive, and its flat mechanics gradually turned into a cognitive challenge as I felt compelled to constantly search for patterns and words. ## Looking at a qualitative research study of an educational technology

I chose *Field Experiences Using iPads: Impact of Experience on Preservice Teachers’ Beliefs* from the *Journal of Music Teacher Education*.

## Questions, Features, Methodology from the study

**a) What are these preservice teachers perceptions of their experiences using iPads to create music and to teach?**

**b) How do these experiences influence their perceptions of the technology’s effectiveness as a teaching tool?**

This was a qualitative experiential case study that utilized written reflections over a five-week field experience with nine preservice teachers using iPads in elementary music classes. The researchers analyzed the written reflections by identifying patterns and themes using an online qualitative program, allowing them to work individually and collaboratively to reach a negotiated consensus in the data <a href="#ref-2" class="no-underline text-blue-600 hover:underline"><sup>[2]</sup></a>.

## Findings

* Teachers went from generalized excitement about using iPads to specific ways that they could be used pedagogically in teaching music.
* Teachers gained a deeper understanding of how iPads could engage students and increased comfort in using technology.
* Teachers learned to better associate different features of apps towards learning objectives.
* The practical experience using technology resulted in teachers connecting ideas to practice, improving their ability to utilize the technology in their teaching.

## The approach: a good example of qualitative research

I found the methods by which the researchers approached the gathering of qualitative data fascinating as it included:

* Naturally emerging insights (rather than starting with a pre-conceived theory).
* A multistep process for information analysis as described by Moustakas <a href="#ref-4" class="no-underline text-blue-600 hover:underline"><sup>[4]</sup></a>, involving finding key ideas, highlighting patterns in the data, working together (triangulating results between members) to agree on themes, and sharing findings with participants to ensure experiences were accurately represented <a href="#ref-2" class="no-underline text-blue-600 hover:underline"><sup>[2]</sup></a>.
* An examination of the development of technological, pedagogical, and content knowledge (TPACK) related to music education.
* A collaboration between multiple researchers, with a defined theoretical framework building off of existing research.

This study’s goal, “to describe ‘the meaning, structure, and essence of the lived experience . . .’ for this group of preservice teachers” <a href="#ref-3" class="no-underline text-blue-600 hover:underline"><sup>[3]</sup></a>, was an excellent use of a qualitative framework and could not have been measured with any sort of quantitative data analysis in the same way. Measuring the change of beliefs over time in the participants through a statistical analysis, survey, or other method would surely have missed the depth and the story behind how and why beliefs naturally changed.

## 1. Cats and Portals: Video Games, Learning, and Play <Cite n={1} title="Gee (2008)" />

In *Cats and Portals*, Gee argues that well-designed video games operate as powerful learning 
spaces by weaving problem-solving, tool use, and mastery into discovery-driven play. Using the 
game *Portal* as an example, Gee shows how the portal gun functions as a “smart tool” that reshapes 
players’ perception, enabling them to consider new possibilities in their environment much like cats 
exploring the world through embodied experimentation. He extends this argument beyond games to describe 
how young people use digital tools to become amateur experts (Pro-Ams) and develop deep expertise and 
sophisticated literacies outside formal schooling.

Gee’s perspective positions games as both immediate learning experiences and gateways into wider 
ecologies of practice, where players engage in embodied experimentation through 
participation in communities and shared practices. This suggests that the understandings cultivated 
through play can serve as productive starting points for deeper learning.

<Callout title="Burning Question" variant="note">
If games like *Portal* can cultivate amateur experts through embodied experiences, how can we honor and build upon that expertise in classroom settings?
</Callout>


---

## 2. Games as Distributed Teaching and Learning Systems <Cite n={2} title="Gee & Gee (2017)" />

In *Games as Distributed Teaching and Learning Systems*, Gee and Gee move beyond viewing games as 
stand-alone learning environments and instead situate them within broader Distributed Teaching and 
Learning (DTAL) systems made up of people, tools, and social practices. They describe learning as an 
embodied, dialogic process that ultimately unfolds through “conversations with the world,” whereby games act as a platform where ideas can be tested and revised through experience. The authors emphasize that what 
learners take up from games depends heavily on the networks of support, mentorship, and participation 
that surround them.

The ideas in this article represent a shift from Gee’s earlier focus on individual cognition by 
reframing learning as relational and embedded within sociotechnical systems shaped by power, access, and infrastructure. 
Although DTAL systems are described as self-organizing and potentially democratizing, they can just as easily reproduce and intensify existing educational inequities when material and social supports are unevenly distributed.

<Callout title="Burning Question" variant="note">
If learning is increasingly taking place beyond school, what responsibilities do educational institutions have to engage with these systems without absorbing, regulating, or neutralizing the agency and passion that make them effective?
</Callout>

---

## Bridging the Two:

<Card class="bg-gray-50 dark:bg-gray-800 border-l-4 border-l-blue-500 p-6">
While *Cats and Portals* emphasizes learning as an individual process of discovery shaped by tool use 
and perception, *Games as Distributed Teaching and Learning Systems* reframes learning as a relational
 and systemic process embedded within networks of social participation, mentoring, and material support. 
Reading these two articles back-to-back reveals a shift from play as personal transformation to play as a socially mediated practice shaped by power, access, and infrastructure
</Card>

## Context: Buying Lumber at the Hardware Store

For this assignment, I chose to map out how to purchase lumber from the hardware store. 
The context for this would typically be in person at a large box-store like Home Depot, Home Hardware, or Rona type store, and may involve interactions with a range of people including cashiers, contractors, tradespeople, salespeople, or other customers. 

Much like other subcultures, folks who frequent construction supply stores often have recognizable clothing, terminology, and behaviours that, to an outsider, may appear obscure or confusing, but to an insider represent an ideal way of navigating a hardware store. 
The interactions I aim to map intend to illuminate the patterns of language use, shared assumptions, and cultural interactions that often structure the typical purchase of construction supplies from a retail store. 

## Instruction Manual

Here is a step by step breakdown of how to purchase and pick out lumber from the hardware store. 

<Steps>
<Step title="Step 1: Determine Your Plan">
  Decide what you're working on and the type of lumber needed. Make a rough list of how many pieces you might need.
  Take note of the dimensions (Length x Width x Height) of the material needed using imperial measurements (inches and feet).
  Be prepared to describe the materials in technical and/or layman terms (to the best of your ability).

  In this example, let's say we're repairing rotten floor boards and posts on an exterior deck.
  <Callout variant="info" title="Tip: Rough vs. Standard Dimensions">
    In modern construction, often times lumber is not cut to the size it is called. If you measure a “2x4” board, you will find it is actually 1.5” x 3.5”. 
    While 2x4 is the usual name for this type of board, its name does not reflect its true physical reality <Cite n={1} title="Module 1 Notes - Topic 1.2.3" />.
  </Callout>
</Step>
<Step title="Step 2: To the Store">
  Head to the hardware store. 
  If you know what you are after, speak to a cashier to get started.
  If you are unsure of what materials you need or need to ask questions, speak to someone at customer service or the contractors desk. 
  <Callout variant="info" title="Tip: Speak the Language">
    When ordering materials, it is common to use measurements and terminologies to effectively communicate needs: “I need three eight-foot pressure-treated two-by-sixes and a treated twelve foot six-by-six post.” 
    Avoid highly contextual language and questions when placing your order: “I’m trying to fix the rot on my back deck, what do you think I should use?”
  </Callout>
</Step>
  <Step title="Step 3: The Transaction">
    After ordering lumber, head to the back lumberyard or warehouse with your order. You will generally be expected to select, transport and load your own individual wood yourself.
  <Callout variant="info" title="Tip: Sight the Wood">
   When selecting your pieces there is a ritual called “sighting” whereby people choose the straightest and nicest pieces of wood.
    Where you lift a board, close one eye, and look down the edge to check for “hockey sticking” (warping) or twisting. 
    If you come across "bad" boards during your search you may reject them, but you must re-stack them neatly. Being mindful of this shows consideration towards the shared environment. 
    After selecting your pieces, it is your responsibility to safely load up your vehicle.
  </Callout> 
  </Step>
  </Steps>

      </p>
    </div>
    
    <div class="bg-red-50 dark:bg-red-900/20 p-4 rounded-lg">
      <h4 class="font-bold text-lg mb-2">What if I say something wrong?</h4>
      <p class="text-sm">
        It's ok! Hardware store cashiers are fairly understanding themselves and are often still learning the correct terminology for wood products too.
      While there might be some initial fear of judgment in ordering lumber, the culture of learning present within this subculture values the attempt.
   </p>
    </div>
  </div>


---

## 3. Analysis using the Onion Model

<div class="max-w-xs mx-auto my-6">
<Figure
src="/assets/images/blog/onionculturegraph.jpg"
alt="Diagram of the onion model of culture showing values at the core and layers of rituals, heroes, and data extending outward"
caption="The ‘onion’ model of culture (Hofstede, 1991 and others)."
/>
</div>

In considering Hofstede’s “Onion Model,” we can analyze the process of buying lumber as a broader multi-layered system.
The outer layer consists of data: symbols and language. Measurements, shorthand, slang, and other hardware-store idioms often serve as distinguishing characteristics that showcase one's knowledge and competence with something. As it relates to construction culture, the communication of data and symbols follows certain standards; the way one speaks about materials can shift the power dynamic of a conversation, or influence how someone else speaks. 
Some examples that demonstrate the data layer would be knowing to speak about measurements in inches and feet or knowing common stock sizes (8ft, 10ft, 12ft, 14ft, 16ft etc. always using imperial). These serve as cultural characteristics that function more like symbolic rules than simple vocabulary recall.

As related to purchasing lumber, the next layer of this diagram serves to recognize hardware store exemplars and seasoned contractors: the "Heroes". In my example, these would be the folks who have learned and are fluent with hardware store terminology such that that know where things are, what types of construction supplies can be ordered and how, and someone who makes correct decisions with efficiency and decisiveness acting as role models for the culture.

Together, the inner and outer layers of Hofstede’s “Onion Model” include the element of practices that creates an interconnectedness between these sections. Applying this logic to the context of purchasing lumber, there is a clear ritualistic “way of doing” things that is kept alive by culture bearers through the use of cultural information and data.

At the center of the diagram is the heart of the values that drive cultural behaviors and considerations.
 As related to purchasing lumber, values like friendliness and clarity come to mind as being important. Because the transaction of purchasing lumber places the burden on the customer to articulate specific needs and measurements, self-reliance is another value that is highly prized within the hardware-store realm.
That said, questions are always acceptable to most anyone at the hardware store, but it becomes important to realize the limitations of a cashier's knowledge compared to that of a professional trades person. 
Patience, humor, and a sense of collective responsibility and learning are also other values I imagine would apply to the majority of hardware store goers.

---

## 4. Reflection

Mapping out the different cultural characteristics through a process like this, to me, really highlights how high the “barrier to entry” is in certain circumstances.

In addition, through the use of the Onion Model, reflecting on the nuances involved in each of the above steps brings to light the idea that culture is not something we <em>have</em> but something we <em>do</em> by engaging with rituals, data, and heroes of our own subcultures.

#### Data Reliability

While the sources I have chosen provide a good starting point for this research, it is vital to acknowledge potential gaps in institutional reporting.
For example, the 2021 Census carried an 4.7% non-response rate for Prince Rupert, which may have resulted in an underrepresentation of marginalized groups. 
In addition to that, as some of the data referenced relies on self-identification, historical trauma and institutional mistrust may influence potential representations of the true Indigenous demographic.

Finally, it is known that data associated with total indigenous identity may be potentially affected by incomplete information due to outlying reserves and settlements.<Cite n={7} title="Statistics Canada (2022)" />

Initially I found the contrast between municipal and school district data to be quite surprising due to their differences. An epiphany I had through this exercise was the sheer amount of variables, interconnectedness, and considerations that go into demographics, data and diversity.
Prior to this assignment, my understanding of Prince Rupert's Indigenous demographics primarily came from experiences in classrooms and informal conversations with colleagues. Looking this data and cross referencing it revealed patterns I had sensed anecdotally but not fully understood in terms of a broader municipal context.

This research illuminated a surprising intersection between high rates of Indigenous identity and poverty in Prince Rupert's educational landscape.
As for Conrad's 78% Indigenous enrollment, it is interesting to consider whether the high percentage is indicative of lower-income neighborhoods associated with the East side of Prince Rupert who may which face barriers to school choice that other more affluent families would not experience (ie. Taking the ferry to/from school everyday)


All and all, reflecting on this data, it is clear to me that part of my role as an educator here in Prince Rupert is to facilitate spaces where students can engage with and explore their cultural identity.

### Formal Elements

Carcassonne's underlying system and mechanics of the game centers around tile placement and resource management. Players randomly draw landscape tiles that they must connect and match to existing tiles and terrain. After placing a tile, players can choose to claim a landscape "feature" to build off of. As Fullerton had noted in chapter 6, formal elements constitute "the underlying system and mechanics of the game" (p. 189). <Cite n={1} title="Fullerton (n.d.)" /> The limited supply of game pieces, along with the requirement to match landscape tiles together strategically creates fun but tense gameplay. The scoring system reinforces this tension by returning game pieces to players only when features are completed.

### Dramatic Elements

While Carcassonne doesn't have a pre-scripted story, there is a natural narrative that emerges through the gameplay as players compete to complete or sabotage their opponents' features. The randomness and the territorial complexity that comes with staking claim to landscape features create dramatic stakes where a single tile can shift control or trap opponent pieces in an unfinishable feature.

### Dynamic Elements

Dynamic elements emerge in Carcassonne through the balance of randomness and strategic tile placement each turn. The unpredictable tile draw forces players to consider both long-term and short-term placement of their game pieces in order to score the most points. A sense of urgency develops as tiles run low that influences players' strategy towards scoring points.

---

## My Reflection

While it appears quite simple in nature and concept, the formal elements and scoring mechanics of Carcassonne are actually quite complex. The dramatic elements experienced through playing the game come from the excitement of closing a feature and earning points, the friendly competition of preventing an opponent from finishing a section, and the strategy of knowing which features on the tiles do or do not exist. The randomness and unpredictability of each player's tile placement create a fun dynamic that keeps excitement high as features are completed.

## Observing the Learning Process

<div class="max-w-2xl mx-auto my-8">
  <Figure
    src="/assets/images/blog/collegeOfCarcassonne.jpg"
    alt="Students playing Carcassonne"
  />
  <p class="text-center text-sm text-gray-500 mt-2 italic">
    Observing young players navigate Carcassonne's learning process through tactile exploration and pattern recognition.

### First Impressions

For these participants, the learning process was largely a tactile and exploratory process characterized by pattern recognition. Initially as the players began interpreting the different landscape tiles and game pieces, there was some confusion about how to initiate the game and what the different coloured game pieces were for. As the participants began turning over the tiles and building on the various landscape features, the learning process of the game quickly evolved into a collaborative "puzzle-solving" mindset from the group rather than a competitive one. In resisting the urge to jump in and guide the players myself, I was able to take note of how the group utilized visual cues and individual tile artwork to decode the game's formal elements. To me, this suggested that reading the rules were a barrier for this group in the learning process, and therefore the tile art was the way for the game to self-correct and coordinate the user to help the player understand the formal elements of the game (even if the idea of scoring points still remained elusive).

### Analysis of Student Impressions and Engagement

Observing the players first impressions of Carcassonne, I found that participants' decisions were primarily driven by visual cues and collective understandings of the landscape tiles. The player's engagement with the game was highest during the tile placement phase each round when a player had to determine the orientation of a tile and connect it to an existing tile. The act of joining the pieces seemed to provide a dramatic sense of suspense, excitement, and accomplishment for each player during their turn. However, the delay between placing a tile and seeing momentum on the scoreboard caused these young players to overlook the competitive elements of the game, leading them to focus instead on the creative aspect of building the map. Without reading the instructions before learning to play the game, the collective impression of Carcassonne from this group was that it was more of a "building toy" in formal structure than a competitive game. This led to an interesting observation of how a game's visual qualities, combined with its formal elements, influence how players perceive and engage with learning a game in a social context. The learning process observed by these young players reinforces how a game's aesthetic and tactile qualities are not merely secondary considerations of a game, but fundamental towards how a player understands and gives meaning to more complex and dynamic elements.

      <p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed">
        I decided to venture into the basement where I found the water main was leaking and flooding the area.
        I was instructed to turn off the power at the breaker panel first, while standing in the flooded basement, which I thought was a bit questionable from a safety standpoint.
        After that I was directed to pick up a pipe wrench and tighten the leaking water main pipes. The game had a visual indicator of a crescent wrench moving in the direction of which to turn the pipe, "righty tighty lefty loosey," which was a nice touch.
      </p>
      <p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed">
        What the player was able to carry at one time was worth noting as the game only allowed for the player to hold one primary tool at a time.
        This meant that in order to pick up a shovel, I had to drop the pipe wrench. This gameplay logic created a back-and-forth that felt realistic in a tedious sort of way.
      </p>
      <p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed">
        In the backyard, I was directed to dig a path for the water so that it could flow to my neighbour's backyard and away from my own home.
        Throughout the first level, tools would magically appear in convenient areas when needed, as if I had a secret labourer doing the hard work for me.
        A generator appeared in the backyard out of nowhere, a submersible pump and extension cord appeared in the basement when I needed them. This happened frequently in the first level, though when I reached the second level the tools were scattered around my pickup truck where I spawned, which felt more like how things actually are on a jobsite.
        For cleaning up the flooded basement, I had to place the submersible pump in the deepest part of the water and run the drain hose up to the backyard. I then had to grab the generator, move it to the entrance of the basement, connect the power cable to the generator, and turn it on. That sequence felt satisfying once it worked. It is worth noting that I did accidentally turn the generator on and connect a live cable to the pump while it was still underwater, which in real life wouldn't have been very fun.
      </p>
      <p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed">
        When I was done with the basement and backyard flooding, I was instructed to find the big squeegee and push the remaining water towards the storm drains on the street.
        When the tutorial ended and I moved onto the second level, "Midtown Market," I felt a bit lost when I was no longer being spoon-fed what to do and was on my own.
      </p>
    </div>

      <p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed">
        I probably spent a good two hours trying to figure out how to rake leaves out of a storm drain. Some of the frustration was primarily due to not being able to see objects as clearly as I needed to due to the lower graphics rendering capabilities of my laptop.
        Discovering that the water level kept gradually rising if I did not fully complete a task added to that challenge, and there were stretches where I genuinely did not know if I was making progress or just moving water around in circles.
      </p>
      <p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed">
        Some satisfying moments came when a task was complete: tightening a pipe, watching the water flow to my neighbour's backyard, and seeing the level complete screen once I managed to eventually "git'r done." By the end of the first level I felt fairly worn out, and moving into the second level without any guidance made that feeling worse before it got better.
      </p>

  <details class="border border-gray-200 dark:border-gray-700 rounded-lg p-4 bg-gray-50 hover:bg-gray-100 dark:bg-gray-800/50 dark:hover:bg-gray-800 open:bg-white dark:open:bg-gray-900 transition-colors">
    <summary class="font-bold cursor-pointer flex items-center gap-1 select-none text-gray-900 dark:text-gray-100">
      <span class="text-lg">Analytic Notes</span>
    </summary>
    <div class="mt-4 space-y-4">
      <p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed">
        It seems that the core problem in each of the levels is always the same: there is water where it should not be, and it is the player's job to move it somewhere else.
        When I finally made it to the second level, I felt that even though I had gone through the process of learning how to use the tools in the first level, I was not really sure where to start when left on my own.
        This made me think that the objective was not really to problem solve, but to follow a patterned way of using tools to mitigate groundwater. In considering this, it seems that the prescribed sequencing of gameplay removes a layer of player agency that a more realistic simulation might have had.
      </p>
      <p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed">
        In each of the tasks, the water level acts as a kind of clock through how it regenerated over time.
        Having an area reflood if the player is not fast enough creates a sense of urgency and makes it so that the player must be efficient in their movements and decisions.
      </p>
      <p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed">
        While I found that the heavy scaffolding in the first level was effective for the initial onboarding of the game, it left me feeling underprepared for the second level when the instruction I had been relying on disappeared and I had to figure out the order of operations on my own.
      </p>
      <p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed">
        The protagonist of the game, being a middle-aged white working-class man, combined with the subtle display of an American flag on his house, the suburban setting, and the disaster scenario, gives the game a fairly specific "lone hero" archetype vibe.
      </p>
      <p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed">
        In addition to this, I found the framing of what was or was not considered dangerous to be worth noting. The character operates electrical equipment while standing in standing water with no PPE and no acknowledgment of real hazards. For a game otherwise trying to simulate trades work in hostile conditions, the absence of any safety framing is a telling omission.
      </p>
      <p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed">
        Another point worth bringing up is the task of redirecting water onto your neighbour's property.
        While it is presented as just another objective, the fact that the game casually glosses over the civic reality of proper drainage solutions is telling of what kind of game this is and the silly workarounds that will be required in order to accomplish various goals.
      </p>
      <p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed">
        Overall, I think the regeneration of debris overtop of the clogged drains was one of my favourite design choices of the game as it showcases the true reality of drainage problems and flowing water: drainage work and drains do not always stay fixed.
      </p>
    </div>

    <summary class="font-bold cursor-pointer flex items-center gap-1 select-none text-gray-900 dark:text-gray-100">
      <span class="text-lg">Summary</span>
    </summary>
    <div class="mt-4 space-y-4">
      <p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed">
        Getting a handle on this game was less about learning the mechanics and more about learning to read the environment well enough to know what needed doing next. The most important skill of this game turned out to be sequencing and knowing what order to tackle the problems in.
      </p>
    </div>

## Section 4: Synthesis
 
<p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed mb-4">
  The most interesting thing about DrainSim is not what it teaches directly, but what it reveals through what it chooses to omit. The missing safety gear, the limitless generator, the magically appearing tools, the casual redirection of floodwater onto a neighbour's property: all of these are moments where the game's version of labour begins to come apart against real-world knowledge and civil complexity. Gee and Gee (2017) describe good gameplay as a process of building a mental model of a system through empirical evidence collected during play, and that is precisely what the game's omissions kept forcing me to do (Gee & Gee, 2017)<Cite n={3} title={"Gee & Gee (2017)"} />.
</p>
 
<p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed mb-4 font-bold">
  <b>Instructionist and Constructionist Scaffolding</b>
</p>
 
<p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed mb-4">
  The first level was undoubtedly instructionist in nature. Every objective was spelled out for the player, every tool had a visual indicator, and the on-screen arrows pointed toward the next task. That kind of hand-holding lowered the barrier to entry at first, but at the expense of player-comprehension. For me, it provided extrinsic motivation to want to clear the drain, but did not build the intrinsic executive function needed to understand why the water continued to rise in the first place (Zainuddin et al., 2020; Alabdulakareem & Jamjoom, 2020)<Cite n={9} title="Zainuddin et al. (2020)" />. As the leaves came back and the water kept rising after already clearing the drains, I did not understand why, because I had been following instructions rather than building knowledge of how the game worked. Gee and Gee (2017) note that players who engage seriously with a game are running rapid cycles of hypothesis, experiment, and analysis (Gee & Gee, 2017)<Cite n={3} title={"Gee & Gee (2017)"} />. The first level's heavy scaffolding short-circuited that cycle for me later leaving me perplexed.
</p>
 
<p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed mb-4">
  This pattern shifted in the next level when the game stopped directing me and started requiring my own independent judgment. Figuring out the order of operations in draining the flooded zones produced a different feeling of success than when I was simply following the on-screen tips. While that was where the real engagement happened for me, that was also where the game's omissions became evident. Without further scaffolding, the simulation's simplified logic around its own tooling became harder to ignore. Gee (2008) argues that the tools given to players in games are designed to change how they approach and interpret an environment, foregrounding certain aspects of the world and deliberately backgrounding others to keep problem-solving simple (Gee, 2008)<Cite n={4} title="Gee (2008)" />. I feel that DrainSim did exactly that.
</p>
 
<p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed mb-4 font-bold">
  <b>Prior Knowledge and Simulation Limitations</b>
</p>
 
<p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed mb-4">
  Coming into this game with residential plumbing experience made certain mechanics click faster. Analogy mapping works best when a player can connect game logic to something they already understand from lived experience (Martin et al., 2019)<Cite n={5} title={"Martin et al. (2019)"} />, and that was often the case. The same prior knowledge made the game's omissions more visible than they might have been to a player without that background.
</p>
 
<p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed mb-4">
  Things like the generator never needing fuel, the character having no PPE despite operating electrical equipment in standing greywater, and the solution to a flooded backyard being to redirect the water onto the neighbour's property were not just minor details to me. These aspects of the game were the difference between an authentic simulation of residential trades work and a gamified depiction of it. In this way, the game quietly frames physical labour as simpler and safer than it is, which is its own kind of ideological statement. Gee (2008) would describe this as the game foregrounding the satisfying, visual side of trades labour while deliberately backgrounding the Pro-Am reality of safety requirements and civic bylaws to keep the gameplay simple and uninterrupted (Gee, 2008)<Cite n={4} title="Gee (2008)" />.
</p>
 
<p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed mb-4 font-bold">
  <b>Distributed Systems and the Digital Divide</b>
</p>
 
<p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed mb-4">
  Watching a Twitch streamer complete the first level made something clear that I had not fully registered during my own playthrough: games are not just software, but texts embedded in distributed systems of hardware, social context, and accumulated game fluency (Gee & Gee, 2017)<Cite n={3} title={"Gee & Gee (2017)"} />. What I experienced as difficult was largely an access problem. The low-end graphics made the environment hard to read, which made decisions harder to make. What looked at first like a learning curve turned out to be partly an inability to see clearly enough to engage with the game the way it was designed.
</p>
 
<p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed mb-4">
  The Twitch session also introduced a social layer the solo playthrough did not have. Taylor (2018) describes live streaming as a form of distributed spectatorship where meaning is co-constructed between the player, the audience, and the game itself, which the chat dynamic illustrated through viewers connecting the flooding scenarios to their own experiences (Taylor, 2018)<Cite n={7} title={"Taylor (2018)"} />. That affinity space dimension added a further interpretive layer that shaped how the streamer and their audience understood the realities associated with the simulation (Gee & Gee, 2017)<Cite n={3} title={"Gee & Gee (2017)"} />.
</p>
 
<p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed mb-4 font-bold">
  <b>Work and Play</b>
</p>
 
<p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed mb-4">
  The game's storyline displays an ideological position about who does this kind of work and why. The protagonist is a middle-aged, working-class white man with a plunger on his belt and an American flag outside his house. The disaster is individual rather than systemic, where one person alone fixes what the municipality could not. The streamer I watched framed this by calling themselves "a true American hero, single-handedly saving the city," and nobody in the chat pushed back. That the framing of this went unchallenged is worth noting: the characterisation of trades labour as a lone, masculine, patriotic endeavour is not an accidental design choice. A more diverse protagonist or a scenario framing the drainage crisis as a collective problem may have told a different story about the same work.
</p>
 
<p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed mb-8">
  The magnet fishing aspect after level two reinforced this individualist logic, where environmental remediation becomes a financial transaction. You fish for trash, you earn money, you spend it on tools. The civic dimension of drainage work quietly disappears here. Zainuddin et al. (2020) describe how extrinsic reward systems, things like in-game currency and unlockable tools, redirect a player's motivation away from any intrinsic value a task might hold and towards a loop of personal accumulation (Zainuddin et al., 2020)<Cite n={9} title="Zainuddin et al. (2020)" />. What began as saving a town becomes transactionary, where the game's overall picture of labour ends up being about the individual rather than the community.
</p>

## Observation Focus

My inquiry question for this assignment was: how does a community organized around a digital currency develop a culture of belonging, and mutual care amongst members which transcends financial premise? I was drawn to this example of digital culture because it appeared to defy the typical stereotype of cryptocurrency communities. Rather than being profit-driven, it appears to be about playful generosity, pseudonymous altruism, and a social space where internet culture and everyday life converged freely in a low-stakes environment.

As Wellman (2011) <Cite n={7} title="Wellman (2011)" /> argues, the Third Age of internet research treats digital spaces as infrastructure we inhabit rather than visit. For many members of the Jungle, I would say this rings true. While technically a cryptocurrency forum, the 48-hour observation window revealed a space where members greet each other by name alias, share photos of pets, meals, and personal interests through community bot commands and user submissions, while also sustaining interest-based conversations across continents and time zones. It became evident to me that the social glue here is not the financial value of the currency, but the digital friendship and internet-culture experienced in real time.

## Observed Events

<div class="my-8">
  <div style="float: right; margin: 0 0 1rem 1.5rem; max-width: 280px;">
    <Figure
      src="/assets/images/blog/rugpullsimulator.png"
      alt="BananoBot"
      style="width: 100%; height: 160px; object-fit: cover; border-radius: 0.5rem;"
    />
    <p class="text-xs text-gray-500 italic leading-snug mt-2 text-center">
      The BananoBot and an ode to Banano's cryptocurrency humour <Cite n={2} title="Banano Discord (2026)" />
    </p>
  </div>

  <p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed mb-4">
    The central event of my observation period was the breakdown of BananoBot, the server's automated tipping system. A malfunction caused it to send tips approximately twenty times the intended amount before it was taken offline a day earlier. While this complicated my original inquiry, it created a fascinating involuntary natural experiment: what happens to a community built around a digital currency when that currency can no longer be shared?
  </p>

  <p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed mb-4">
    Rather than frustration, I observed humour, adaptation, and continued generosity through alternative means. During the outage, members attempted to tip one another genuine amounts of 19 BAN (the number 19 being a community inside joke referencing the atomic number of potassium), unaware that the bot was offline. One member reframed the outage as a Star Wars joke. Another posted a mock tip of an absurdly large meme-number to active users, not expecting it to work but performing the gesture anyway. These responses reflect what Macfadyen (2006) <Cite n={6} title="Macfadyen (2006)" /> describes as group ritual "text acts": communicative performances through which members collectively attest to shared identity. Drawing on Ricoeur's concept of attestation, Macfadyen argues that virtual identity is not passively held but actively performed through repeated ritualized acts. One telling moment of this came when a server citizen attempted to tip a non-citizen and the tip failed, to which the recipient responded with genuine gratitude, noting that it was "the gesture that counted" more than the actual occurrence of the tip.
  </p>

  <p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed">
  The community's virtual culture was further recognized when some members came to the realization that the BananoBot was down and chose to adapt to another tipbot instead. As an alternative, several citizens switched to the server's Nano tipbot as a workaround, choosing to continue the micro-tip culture through small amounts of a different cryptocurrency rather than BAN. The persistence of the tipping culture amongst the community, even with the malfunctioning bot, reveals an explicit decoupling of social value from a friendly financial transaction.
  </p>

  <div style="clear: both;"></div>
</div>

## Identity Expression and Community Norms

<div class="my-8">
  <div style="float: left; margin: 0 1.5rem 1rem 0; max-width: 240px;">
    <Figure
      src="/assets/images/blog/bananoreacts.png"
      alt="Banano Server Reactions"
    />
    <p class="text-xs text-gray-500 italic leading-snug mt-2 text-center">
      Discord server reactions and emojis unique to Banano and "the-jungle" <Cite n={2} title="Banano Discord (2026)" />
    </p>
  </div>

  <p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed mb-4">
    Despite pseudonymous usernames, members displayed remarkably personal identities within this observational period. One user shared personal landscape photographs; another shared images of a hobby project; a third shared pictures of food. Cheong, Martin and Macfadyen (2012) <Cite n={3} title="Cheong, Martin & Macfadyen (2012)" /> had observed that online communication is always rooted in biographical and physical realities where physical matter is important, even in digital spaces. These moments of real-world influence that appear are thought of to be the substance to which virtual identity becomes felt as genuine amongst users in the community.
  </p>

  <p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed mb-4">
    Platform-specific rituals reinforced this further through: Beatrice-Bot interactions that surfaced community-submitted pet photos on demand (using !pup, and !meow commands), a recurring daily food post from one user which drew warm emoji reactions, and a spontaneous forty-minute conversation between a member from South America and another from Western Europe about concerts from a band both members had previously seen. These interactions are indicative of virtual culture not as inherited biological markers, but as a shared identity constructed through repeated, digital interactions. The food posts, the pet photos, the music conversation all being part of The Jungle's shared customary interactions.
  </p>

  <p class="text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed">
    Another instance of community identity I observed was defined when members collectively protected against the values the group stood for. During my observation period, one member had spotted a fraudulent Banano website appearing in their search results and shared it amongst the group. Members responded quickly and without prompting, reporting the domain within minutes and determining the site functioned as a clipboard-hijacking scam designed to steal cryptocurrency. Although no one was asked to intervene, and no one was rewarded for bringing this to the attention of the group, community members responded immediately to protect the collective. What struck me about this moment was how naturally it reflected the community's broader sense of mutual responsibility, particularly towards newcomers who might fall victim to the scam website. This was a clear example of the community's altruistic cultural-norms extending beyond humour and social warmth into something more actively protective of the community's identity as a whole.
  </p>

  <div style="clear: both;"></div>
</div>
---

## Critical Reflection

In writing this, I felt my main ethical challenge was avoiding the over-romanticization of what I observed. I found it fascinating that such interpersonal cohesion existed among members who may simultaneously be experiencing real financial losses or anxieties due to the volatility of the cryptocurrency's financial value and the stalling of the network. It was particularly interesting to witness the stalled Banano network unfolding in real time while community engagement continued uninterrupted, with no visible skepticism toward the community's overall integrity or values. As Cheong, Martin and Macfadyen (2012) <Cite n={3} title="Cheong, Martin & Macfadyen (2012)" /> note, digital spaces are characterized by fluid and often contradictory tensions. What I observed here was a community navigating those tensions with humour and unsuspecting grace. In a space centered around a digital asset, the most valuable currency appeared to be the attention, humour, and social care exchanged between members which in itself, is a form of collective attestation towards who these people are and what they stand for in this virtual space.

## Hegemonic Game Culture

At its core, it is clear that video game culture is built around (and continues to serve) a narrow demographic of cisgendered, white, male players.
Alongside this acknowledgment comes an inherited assumption: that skill within video game culture is as a masculine trait, and so when women demonstrate exceptionality in video games, the culture doesn't celebrate them, it questions them.

This hegemony surfaces in different ways, three of which particularly stand out to me:

## The Boundaries: How They're Built and Kept

### In Competitive Play of Esports

In the case of Geguri, when she outperformed her male peers in the game called *Overwatch* the community's response wasn't admiration: it was accusations of cheating. <Cite n={2} title="Choi, Slaker & Ahmad (2020)" /> 
Even after proving her abilities through a live demonstration, the attacks shifted to her appearance, her age, and her reluctance to be called a "feminist icon." Her skill wasn't enough in this regard, she also had to justify her right to simply exist in the space.

This is common barrier that women in esports face: they are surveilled, questioned, and required to demonstrate their legitimacy in ways men never are. <Cite n={11} title="Witkowski (2018)" /> 
Another recent empirical study by Crothers, Scott-Brown, and Cunningham (2024) confirms that these experiences are not isolated: women in esports report pervasive harassment that range from verbal abuse, to doxing, even sexual-related threats, after which many participants eventually leaving competitive spaces altogether. <Cite n={3} title="Crothers, Scott-Brown & Cunningham (2024)" />


### In the Industry Itself

Problems don't stop at the player level, but the industry itself also contains undignified workplace cultures that have become protected ecosystems of harm where powerful men face no accountability and HR structures are compromised by personal relationships, where women who ended up raising concerns did so at the risk of their careers. <Cite n={9} title="Schreier (2020)" /> 
This sends a powerful message to women considering a career in games: they are not safe within these organizations.

### Through Tropes and Representation

The "girl gamer" label strips women of individual identity and assigns them a corner of the culture they're permitted to occupy, generally as long as they remain palatable to male audiences. This is often seen within games themselves, where women are frequently flattened into stereotypical roles such as: the healers, the love interest, the reward at the end of the game. 

As a test, I decided to investigate similar prompts of "male/female gamer" and "male/female video game character" to cross examine how different AI models reveal these cultural biases.

## Context: A Virtual Breakdown

For this assignment, I chose to analyze a workplace communication breakdown that occurred between two teaching professionals coordinating a organization-wide event.
This incident took place entirely over email, transforming what could have been a simple scheduling clarification into an escalating conflict that involved accusations of unprofessionalism and the eventual intervention of a senior colleague.

Let's call these two teaching professionals **Jordan** and **Karen**. 

What makes this case particularly interesting is that neither party was wrong according to their own cultural frameworks. Jordan, newer to the organization, valued explicit coordination and advance notice. Karen, a long-standing professional, operated within an established tradition where certain steps were understood without being stated.

 Jordan responded again to clarify that he had never said he would not participate.

## 3. Analysis Using Neuliep's Contextualized Model

<div class="max-w-md mx-auto my-6">
<Figure
src="/assets/images/blog/Neuliep.jpg"
alt="Diagram of Neuliep's Contextualized Model showing Perceptual Cultural Context, Environmental Context, and Socio-Relational Context layers surrounding the communication process"
caption="Neuliep's Contextualized Model of Intercultural Communication (adapted from Neuliep, 2015)"
/>
</div>

Neuliep's <Cite n={2} title="Module 2 Notes (ETEC 542)" /><Cite n={3} title="Neuliep, 2015" /> model provides a framework for understanding how multiple layers of context influence communication outcomes. In this case, three contexts interacted to produce the breakdown.

### Perceptual Cultural Context: A Clash of Micro-Cultures

At the heart of this incident was a mismatch between two professional micro-cultures:

**Jordan's Cultural Filter (Low-Context, Protocol-Based):** <Cite n={1} title="Module 1 Notes (ETEC 542)" />
- Explicit coordination = professional respect and collaboration
- Advance notice = respect for autonomy and opinion,
- Information must always be stated, not assumed
- Consultation before public announcements is mandatory

**Karen's Cultural Filter (High-Context, Tradition-Based):** <Cite n={1} title="Module 1 Notes (ETEC 542)" />
- Twenty-year tradition = the advertisement of this event itself should have been sufficient notice
- Shared institutional knowledge is assumed 
- The Context itself carries meaning (without stating it)
- Continuity of tradition = professional efficiency

<Callout variant="info" title="Neuliep on Ethnocentrism">
  Neuliep (2015) <Cite n={3} title="Neuliep, 2015, p. 45" /> argues that "Ethnocentrism involves judging another culture exclusively by the standards of one's own culture." 
  Both Karen and Jordan were evaluating the other's behavior through their own cultural lenses. Karen saw Jordan's question as a violation of tradition and rejection of "how things traditionally are", while Jordan saw Karen's expectation and assumption of traditional knowledge as a violation of professionally courteous communication protocols.
</Callout>
### Environmental Context: The Medium Became the Problem

The email format played a crucial role in the escalation. As Reeder et al. (2004) <Cite n={5} title="Reeder et al., 2004" /> observe, electronically mediated communication lacks "dynamic real-time repair mechanisms" and in-the-moment tools that can stop a misunderstanding from becoming sustained conflict.

### Socio-Relational Context: Power, Social-Status, and Policing

Another layer on this incident worth sharing involves the social dynamics and experiences between the communicators. 
Karen held twenty years of institutional knowledge working in the local community. This is cultural capital that Jordan did not yet possess as this was second time ever participating in the event (the first time, prior communication before the event had taken place months beforehand through a previous coordinator who was no longer with the organization after Jordan's first year). As it might have been Karen's first time communicating for herself in organizing this traditional event, perhaps she did not know how or believed that Jordan what she knows about how it typically functions. <Cite n={4} title="Plum, 2007" />
When Karen CC'd their response to senior administration calling Jordan unprofessional, it might have been experienced by him not as a request for support, but as cultural policing tactic to support her own stance rather than acknowledge any miscommunication.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Question to Consider">
  When does CC'ing a administration help a situation, and when does it police it? While the same action of including a supervisor can be read as an act of inclusion and transparency by one party, it can be an act of intimidation by another.
</Callout>

Karen's labelling of Jordan's communication as "unprofessional" demonstrates what Neuliep <Cite n={3} title="Neuliep, 2015" /> calls cultural policing which attempts to regulate whose norms count as the default. The term "unprofessional" in itself is particularly powerful because it neutralizes and dismisses one's perspective while enforcing a cultural standard of "what is professional" onto others.

---
## 4. Reflection

What strikes me most about this case is how easily a simple misunderstanding escalated into something much larger, essentially trapping both parties in their initial interpretations, with no real path back to the original conversation.

This situation serves as a good reminder that virtual communication is never neutral. In an email format, cultural and microcultural contexts, combined with environmental and perceptual filters, shape the entire socio-relational context of an interaction before either party has a chance to mitigate any misunderstandings. <Cite n={2} title="Module 2 Notes (ETEC 542)" /> Without the ability to hear tone, clarify intent, or ask "Wait, what did you mean by that?" in real time, each response hardened the conflict rather than resolved it. By the time Karen CC'd Jordan's administration, neither party was communicating about the original issue anymore. They were defending their own professionalism and their own version of what had happened.

What I keep coming back to is not just the breakdown of communication itself, but rather what became as a result of it. The refusal to mediate misunderstandings, the sustained misinterpretations, and the real-world consequences that followed Jordan as a result of this virtual miscommunication. 
- **In shared professional spaces, whose cultural norms get to count as the default, how is that established, and what happens to everyone else?**
- **At what point does "this is how we do things" stop being an orientation and start being a barrier to one's autonomy? - How might individuals negotiate their agency within shared traditions?**
- **What happens when you're expected to carry a tradition or cultural norms that were never yours to begin with?**

## My Contribution

Collaborating on the game *Undercooked* over the past few weeks with my teammates Jordan Swallow, Noralee Yarra, Dorlen Gomez and Nancy Peters was a humbling experience. My contributions within our game centered on designing the introductory page, the narrative, and the audio elements for our creation. As these are the first elements a player encounters, it led me to reflect on the impact initial impressions create even before they engage with the mathematical or culinary game mechanics.

Fullerton (2019) had noted that a strong game concept begins with a compelling premise that grounds players in the logic and context of play. <Cite n={1} title="Fullerton (2019)" /> By establishing a simple, cross-cultural narrative that contextualized the game as a chef working in a global foods festival, we created a naturally unfolding situation where cultural and mathematical opportunities collide through unique culinary requests. This connects to my previous Intellectual Productions on representation and how inclusion can either deepen or undercut educational potential depending upon the game's core systems. By featuring global cuisines and centering student identity amongst the challenges of measuring and assembling ingredients, we followed through with meaningful design choices that enhanced the game's overall scope and systems of play.


## Technical Challenges

Utilizing Genially for this project presented some trade-offs worth bringing up. While the learning curve of the graphical interface was initially a deterrent for me, the platform facilitated seamless real-time collaboration among my team with varying technical backgrounds. While Genially's technical limitations grew into a barrier in manifesting what we had envisioned, we were able to provide enough of a foundation to show how our game could be implemented if we had chosen an alternative tech-stack. Fullerton (2019) also described meaningful play as a condition where player choices lead to outcomes that are both discernible and integrated into the larger system. <Cite n={1} title="Fullerton (2019)" /> Unable to fully demonstrate the scoring and variable management we had envisioned, we could only gesture towards what that would look like within our game rather than physically encode it.


## Designing for Meaningful Outcomes

Reflecting on my field notes of the game *DrainSim*, *Undercooked* provided a sharper lens for analyzing our work. Gee (2008) describes games as environments that foreground specific aspects of a problem while backgrounding others to keep play manageable. <Cite n={2} title="Gee (2008)" /> *DrainSim*'s early levels were so heavily guided that I found myself simply following instructions rather than developing my own internal logic. I initially worried that *Undercooked* risked falling into the same trap: without true variable-based systems, student choices remain merely cosmetic rather than systemically impactful. With more development time, rebuilding the prototype in a browser-native framework could enable dynamic, individualized logic. This would ensure outcomes are genuinely tied to students' reasoning, rather than simply rewarding procedural compliance.

Gee and Gee (2017) describe learning as a process of conversations with the world, testing, revising, and building understanding across tools and contexts. <Cite n={3} title="Gee & Gee (2017)" />
*Undercooked* reached for this dialogue. While the premise provided the right foundation, a foundation only matters if the system allows for the complexity of building on top of it. That is the work I would go back and finish.

## 1. Setting the Scene

I teach Grade Two in Prince Rupert, BC, in a school where the majority of my students are First Nations. I have played guitar most of my life and have held various music education roles across elementary and secondary schools, and the question I keep returning to is whether I should be engaging with First Nations music and storytelling at all, and if so, whether I could ever do that authentically as a non-Indigenous person trained in another musical tradition myself?

To give this some context, before my time in Prince Rupert, I had worked for a few years in villages north of Terrace (Laxgalts'ap, Gitwinksihlkw, and Gingolx) and played in the Majagaleehl Nisga'a community band on Nisga'a Territory. In this previous teaching role, a longstanding community band member gave me permission to use a First Nations melody with my beginner band class and provided a notated copy of the melody. I took this and arranged it for my elementary concert band students and then had them play it as part of their repertoire. All I had been told about this melody was that it was a spring song. Looking back, I am still not sure what to make of this experience. While I had permission to do this, I didn't have much context about it at the time. I was able to take something small that had been shared with me and make it into something I could then share with my students. Given that I was living in this village at the time, was this a responsible inclusion or just a more well-intentioned version of indigenous decorated music education?

That moment prompts this question for me: as a music educator working predominantly with First Nations students, I want to know what genuine place-based inclusion actually looks like as a non-indigenous educator. Is this something I can actually approach with honestly from my own musical training within the limits of what I actually have standing to do?

---

## 2. Connecting to Course Learning

My question about whether I was decorating a Western model with Indigenous elements connects directly to some of the content and discussions I encountered in this course. Looking back at my arrangement of the Nisga'a spring song, I can now see that even with permission and good intentions, I may have been imposing Indigenous knowledge into an essentialist framework of Western music education.

<Cite n={1} title="Rogers (2006)" /> points out that cultural appropriation is complex, and that the political implications of borrowing cultural artifacts are not entirely determined by the intentions of the person doing the borrowing. This was a point that clicked for me. Even with active permission, I had borrowed this cultural material and translated it into a format that made sense by my Western standards for my band students.

Connecting this instance further to the course through our learning about Hawaiian pedagogy of aloha, where teaching is rooted in kuleana, mutual trust, and community, <Cite n={2} title="Gunawardena et al. (2019)" /> helped me to rationalize and let go of the idea that I needed to be an authority on traditions that are not mine.

---

## 3. Describing My Application

In my reflection of this, the spring song arrangement is actually an interesting benchmark for how I might approach indigenous music education content differently in the future. I don't think the problem was the melody itself necessarily or that I had used it with permission, perhaps what I could have done differently would be to be more mindful about the context and background I had shared along aside it when I taught it. In addition to that, having arranged this music myself after only being given a written melody, perhaps I should have reapproached the community member that shared it with me and sought their advice on what I come up with as if to understand the cultural framework behind it and if there was a time or way to teach or share it.

Alongside that point, perhaps to engage students further with music learning, I could have taught the music for this by ear through my voice, rather than notation, or through call and response to engage watching and imitating rather than through reading.

Another thought about this, would have been to invite the community member themselves into my class as if to have this act of music learning become more sharedly collaborative and involved from them. I could have created the community-based co-design conditions where the knowledge keeper themselves were actually present in the room given our friendship had I thought about that at the time.

---

## 4. Reflecting on Significance and Future Directions

One of the biggest shifts for me through reflecting on this experience and my question of how I could genuinely include indigenized music content into my own teaching practice now, has been realizing that things aren't always about permission. While I had that, what I didn't necessarily have was a full understanding of context, collaboration, or any real understanding of the cultural framework behind it on my part. That's what I'd like to keep working on in the future within my teaching practice.

That said, I know I’ll still be reflecting on how to build genuine relationships with knowledge keepers in my community, so I can create opportunities to engage more deeply with content outside my cultural comfort zone. I suppose that my next step would be to open up further place-based conversations with the network of people I have in my community, and explore the wisdom that remains around me hidden in plain sight.

## Artifact
The artifact I choose is my old acoustic "campfire" guitar. It has a crack through the face of it, a painter's tape patch covering a hole filled with wood fill, a misfit G-string tuner/machine head, a turquoise bunny guitar strap my friend made, and to top everything off, the high E and B strings are currently strung on the wrong machine heads (the B string broke previously and this was my quick solution). There really isn't anything special about this guitar I'd say. Its purpose to me was always to have a guitar I could bring anywhere and share with friends while being something I wouldn't worry about if it got damaged by accident.

## Explicit Curriculum
Having this guitar in various contexts taught me to really listen in on my surroundings and consider how my own sound fits within that audible space. Whether that meant playing tunes with people, being wallpaper music, performing a song for people, or playing along with other music, the guitar was a tool for thinking about how one fits into the overall bigger picture. This would be related to skills like musicality, music theory, musicianship, and ear training.

## Implicit Curriculum
Because I never worried about this guitar's aesthetics, I felt free to take risks with it in ways I wouldn't with an instrument I cared more about. Having greater casualness about it allowed me to relax more when playing and sharing it with others, thereby lowering the stakes and entry point towards talking about music or playing guitar. On a more implicit level I think this guitar created the space for creative bi-directional risk taking between myself and others just by having it around. "Ever heard Happy Birthday in minor? Neither have I. Let's try it!" In this sense the guitar is what Pinar might call a site of lived existential experience <Cite n={2} title="The method of currere" />, something that shaped how I understood myself as a learner in relation to others without ever announcing itself as curriculum. I suppose this is also to say that having it around reminded me that music means different things to different people, and our relationship to an instrument as a player or listener can reflect that.

## Reflection
Having an instrument that doesn't matter materialistically opened up new perspectives for me towards how it could be used to engage others through their own relation to music. I think this has come to help me shape my own thoughts towards how playing music is something that can be alive and doesn't always have to be perfect. The researchers in the Currere Cymru piece <Cite n={1} title="Our currere journey" /> found that their most significant curriculum moments centred around belonging and identity, and I think that's true of this guitar too, for me and the people around it. In a similar sense, it is interesting to reflect on how I can create those same conditions of openness and invitation in my own teaching and classroom that this guitar creates for me and my philosophy towards music education.

# Personal Definition


In my current work as an elementary teacher, I think of slop as something that happens when computer generation replaces human judgment. One working definition of Slop could be something to the likes of whether anyone who has a knowledge of, care towards, or an interest in the outcome, took the time to review and approve the work before sharing it. Slop is the gap between design, accuracy, and mindful intent, whether obvious or subtle.

Jones et al. (2025) write about "careless speech," <Cite n={1} title="Careless speech" /> which I felt resonated with my definition. Slop is more than "low quality" content; it is content created with an indifference towards the outcome. In teaching elementary-aged students in particular, that indifference carries a high cost in learning outcomes since younger children may not have the ability to discern or question something presented to them as fact. 

This sentiment is also carried through to the Buckingham (2019) reading, where the question is raised: "who pays the price for how this technology is used in the classroom?" <Cite n={2} title="The Media Education Manifesto" /> in relation to Slop. To me, Slop is computer generated material that lacks oversight and judgement without any human care behind it.

For my Week 8 Doings post, I took a lead sheet of a jazz standard and shifted its modality into an interactive music-education/art game. <i>Cotton Tail</i> was what I ended up with. It is a game where the player (Bunny Webster) follows, imitates, and trades movement phrases with a mentor rabbit (The Duke). As a nod to the great Jazz Musicians Duke Ellington and Ben Webster, <i>Cotton Tail</i> serves as a precursor to jazz education, through an interactive entanglement of jazz history and aural pedagogy. 

The game is built with Phaser and the Web Audio API, and it's designed to have the user experience call-and-response, improvisation, AABA form, and observational skills through spatial gameplay rather than music notation or other written literacies. Together, two rabbits take the player on a natural journey progressing through a cycle of learning, following, imitating, internalizing, experimenting, and creating.

The idea behind translating this song into another modality began as a pun, but it grew into a chance to examine how gamification can support "musicking" (simultaneously celebrating, listening to, and producing music) alongside other media. By inviting the user to follow, imitate, and eventually carve their own path through the game, I have been able to represent the essence of what it means to teach, learn, and engage with music concepts through the transmutation of spatial improvisation.

## Reflection

<div class="md:float-left md:w-1/4 md:mr-6 md:mb-4 w-3/4 sm:w-1/2 mx-auto md:mx-0 mb-6">
  <Figure
    src="/assets/images/blog/cottontail-sheet.png"
    alt="Lead sheet excerpt of Duke Ellington's Cotton Tail"
  />
</div>

**What did the original mode assume about who could access it?** Engaging with "Cotton Tail" in its original form assumes a player proficient enough on an instrument to navigate the chord progression, in print or by ear. That said, knowing how to decode the chord symbols and melody alone doesn't mean someone can play the tune with others or improvise freely over it, since those are separate skills the sheet assumes of the user. Kress <Cite n={2} title="Multimodality" /> argues that a curriculum built on one linear mode of representation can "entrench inequality" and privilege learners who already have access to that mode over those who don't.

**What was carried across in the translation, and what was lost?** Transforming this into a game meant leaving behind the melody in favor of the tune's underlying harmony and the AABA format holding the song together. I also chose to highlight the iterative, mentor-guided learning of following, imitating, and improvising that characterizes an oral tradition. While I used AI throughout this process to help put together my artifact, I had to navigate its own register of authority and foreclosures rather than treat it as a neutral tool, especially around tempo versus time and the gestural cues I tried to embody in the game. What I ended up with reminds me of what Christine Sun Kim described in her TED talk: sight and sound functioning simultaneously, like a chord, rather than sequentially, the way notes on a page might be read one at a time <Cite n={5} title="The enchanting music of sign language" />.

  />

</div>

**What does this reveal about the bodyminds the curriculum was designed for, and those it was not?** My transfiguration of Cotton Tail reveals that the original curriculum assumed a bodymind capable of solitary, page-based decoding, disadvantaging those who learn relationally and through other multimodal means <Cite n={1} title="DisCrit Solidarity" />. In a musical tradition built on listening, imitation, creation, and improvisation, a print modality can't capture the rich history and performative depth possible beyond the page. Moving this artifact away from a modality of isolated, individual mastery brings out the holistic spirit of performative arts, where bodyminds rely on each other's gestural cues and interdependence in real time.

---
title: "Reasonix: An AI Writing Workflow That Knows When to Shut Up"
description: "What my blog's agent workflow actually does, how it encodes a writing voice into machine-readable rules, and where the whole thing falls apart."
publishDate: "2026-07-23"
tags: ["ai", "writing", "tools"]
draft: false
---

import Prose from "../../components/blocks/Prose.astro";
import Cite from "../../components/widgets/Cite.astro";
import Figure from "../../components/blocks/Figure.astro";
import CapabilitiesLimits from "../../components/widgets/CapabilitiesLimits";

<Prose>

<Figure
  src="https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1455390582262-044cdead277a?w=1200&q=80"
  alt="A hand holding a pen over paper, mid-sentence, with natural light across the desk."
/>

I have been experimenting with something quietly for months. It sits in a directory called `.reasonix/` at the root of this blog's repo. Most readers will never see it. It is not a product. It is not a startup. It is a set of Markdown files that tell an AI how to write like me.

The experiment started with a question I could not shake: can you encode a distinctive writing voice into machine-readable instructions well enough that an AI can draft, edit, and even publish in that voice without flattening it?

I did not expect the answer to be yes. And it is not. Not fully. But the partial yes is more interesting than any full no I had assumed going in.

The system is called Reasonix. This post is about what it actually is, what it can actually do, and where it breaks down. No hype. No "AI is the future of writing" hand-waving. Just the real setup and the real problems.

## What Reasonix Actually Is

Reasonix is not an application. It is a directory of agent definition files that an AI coding assistant reads before doing anything on this blog.

The directory structure looks like this:

```
REASONIX.md                          # shared context, hard safety rules
.reasonix/
  skills/
    blog-writer/
      SKILL.md                       # the writing subagent definition
      STYLE_GUIDE.md                 # 20 voice rules, 4 voice traits
    astro-dev/
      SKILL.md                       # the dev subagent
    frontmatter/
      SKILL.md                       # frontmatter generator
    media/
      SKILL.md                       # image/media handler
  commands/
    new-post.md                      # scaffold a draft
    publish.md                       # build, commit, push
```

Each file is a system-level instruction set. When I invoke the writing subagent, it reads `REASONIX.md` first for shared context, then `STYLE_GUIDE.md` for the voice rules, then its own `SKILL.md` for role-specific behavior. Only then does it touch a word.

The rules are not vague. Rule 10: no em dashes. Rule 4: cut hedging phrases like "I feel that" and "I believe that." Rule 15: every citation carries the author's take immediately after it. Rule 3: do not define jargon the author left undefined. The style guide runs to 180 lines with a quick-reference banned-phrase list, before/after examples, and a conflict resolution order.

This level of specificity is not an aesthetic choice. It is load-bearing architecture. Without it, the system defaults to generic AI-written prose. With it, something closer to my actual voice emerges.

The prompt engineering literature has a term for this kind of layered instruction: Schulhoff et al. (2024) catalogued 58 distinct prompting techniques in their systematic survey and found that the most effective strategies combine multiple techniques into highly specific instruction sets rather than relying on any single one. <Cite n={1} title="The Prompt Report" /> Reasonix is not one prompt. It is a stack of them. Each constrains a different dimension of the output.

## What It Can Actually Do

Four things. Each is real, but each comes with a catch.

**Draft in voice.** Give the writing subagent an outline or a set of notes and it will produce a draft that reads like me. Not perfectly. But close enough that editing takes minutes instead of hours. The agent applies all 20 rules, checks for the four named moves (THINKING-ALOUD, COMMIT-BIT, JARGON-TRUST, CITE-THEN-TAKE), and defaults to the tighter 2026 register. It then runs a self-check before returning anything.

<Figure
  src="https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1517694712202-14dd9538aa97?w=1200&q=80"
  alt="A laptop screen glowing in a dim room with code visible, suggesting a development or writing environment."
/>

**Edit against voice rules.** Feed it an existing paragraph that has drifted into academic hedging or corporate filler and the subagent will diagnose the specific violations by rule number, then rewrite at the smallest possible scope. It will not "improve" the paragraph. It will fix rule 4, or rule 18, or rule 19, and leave everything else alone. This is the hardest discipline to enforce in AI-assisted writing, and it is the one that matters most. The subagent's mandate reads: "Do not rewrite a paragraph that only needs one sentence cut."

**Scaffold posts.** The `new-post` command reads 3-5 existing posts to match frontmatter conventions, generates a kebab-case slug, creates the `.mdx` file with `draft: true`, writes a description under 160 characters, suggests tags reusing the existing vocabulary, and creates the image directory. Then it stops and tells me what to do next. It does not write body text. It does not guess what the post is about.

**Build, commit, push.** The `publish` command runs `pnpm build` first. If the build fails, it stops cold and reports the errors. On a green build, it stages everything, generates a commit message from the changed files (format: `post: <slug>: <one-line summary>`), commits, and pushes. Netlify auto-deploys on push. The agent then tells me what was committed, what the message was, confirms the push succeeded, and reminds me how to revert. Rule 3 from REASONIX.md: "Agents may publish or push a post live without waiting on review first, but must always inform the author immediately when they do: what changed, where it's live, and how to revert it."

All of this works. Not flawlessly. But it works often enough to be useful. The key insight is that none of these capabilities is novel on its own. Every AI writing tool on the market can draft a blog post. What makes Reasonix different is that it does not stop at drafting. It layers drafting, editing, scaffolding, and publishing into a single workflow where each step is constrained by the same voice rules, the same safety rules, and the same verification steps.

## The Constraint Paradox

Here is where it gets interesting.

The more rules I add to STYLE_GUIDE.md, the more consistent the output becomes. Rule 10 (no em dashes) is trivially enforceable. Rule 4 (no hedging) catches 90% of what makes AI prose sound like AI prose. Rule 19 (no corporate vocabulary: "utilize" becomes "use," "facilitate" becomes "help") eliminates an entire register that most LLMs default to.

But more rules also create new failure modes. The subagent sometimes over-applies a rule in a context where it does not belong. A sentence that genuinely needs a long breath gets chopped into fragments because rule 8 says sentences should mostly stay under 25 words. A paragraph that actually benefits from a transitional phrase gets stripped because rule 18 says no filler transitions. The rules are blunt instruments. They do not understand nuance. They apply themselves mechanically.

This is the constraint paradox. Patricia Stokes spent a career studying it. In *Creativity from Constraints: The Psychology of Breakthrough*, she argued that constraints do not merely limit creative output. They structure the problem space. <Cite n={2} title="Creativity from Constraints" /> A painter who restricts herself to a single brush makes different decisions than one with a full studio. A sonnet's fourteen lines do not inhibit the poet; they give the poet something to push against.

The same dynamic operates in Reasonix, but with a twist. The constraints are not self-imposed by the writer. They are imposed by a second system that does not understand *why* a given constraint exists. The style guide knows that em dashes are banned, but not why I banned them. It knows that COMMIT-BIT paragraphs must not be undercut, but it does not know when something is or is not a COMMIT-BIT. It pattern-matches. Sometimes it matches wrong.

What I have learned is that the voice rules work best as a backstop, not as a generative engine. When I draft a paragraph myself and ask the subagent to tighten it, the result is almost always better. The rules catch what I miss. When I ask the subagent to draft from scratch, the result is usable but requires heavier editing. The rules cannot supply what they are designed to protect. They can only protect what is already there.

## Where It Breaks Down

Five failure modes. Each one tells you something about what these systems are actually doing under the hood.

**Failure 1: It cannot originate an idea.** Reasonix can draft a post from notes. It cannot decide what is worth writing about. The subagent has no access to my experiences, my classroom, my students, or my internal sense of what is interesting. It can work with what I give it. It cannot generate what I have not yet noticed. This is the largest gap, and it is structural. No amount of voice rules will close it.

**Failure 2: It cannot verify a fact.** REASONIX.md rule 1 says: "Never invent a fact, statistic, citation, or quote." The subagent follows this. But following it means it cannot independently check whether something I wrote is accurate. If I claim something false in my notes, the agent will draft it into the post without objection. It is a style enforcer, not a fact-checker.

**Failure 3: It over-applies rules in edge cases.** The self-check at the end of every draft is a checklist. Checklists do not exercise judgment. A sentence that needs to be 30 words for rhythm will get flagged by rule 8. A rhetorical move that looks like hedging but is actually a deliberate gesture of uncertainty gets flattened into a declarative. The subagent's conflict resolution clause says clarity beats voice, but it cannot reliably tell when clarity has been served and when it has been sacrificed.

**Failure 4: It cannot sustain a COMMIT-BIT from scratch.** The COMMIT-BIT is the hardest move to automate. It requires setting up something slightly absurd and treating it with total seriousness across multiple paragraphs without ever winking at the reader. The subagent can preserve an existing COMMIT-BIT. It can edit one without undercutting it. But generating one cold, with the right premise, the right pacing, and the right restraint, almost never lands. The humor comes out forced or the commitment wobbles. This is not a prompt engineering problem. It is a fundamental limitation of a system that has never actually found anything funny.

**Failure 5: The system drifts when it is not re-grounded.** Each invocation of the subagent reads STYLE_GUIDE.md fresh. In theory, this means it never forgets the rules. In practice, long drafting sessions across multiple invocations produce subtle drift. A rule gets applied slightly differently. A register choice shifts from tighter to looser. The subagent does not have memory of its own previous decisions, so it cannot calibrate against them. The consistency is invocation-level, not session-level.

<Figure
  src="https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1555949963-aa79dcee981c?w=1200&q=80"
  alt="A close-up of tangled wires and cables, representing the complexity and potential points of failure in interconnected systems."
/>

## The Architecture That Makes It Work

Under the hood, Reasonix follows what Anthropic (2024) calls a workflow pattern, as distinct from a fully autonomous agent. <Cite n={3} title="Building effective agents" /> Workflows are "systems where LLMs and tools are orchestrated through predefined code paths." Agents are "systems where LLMs dynamically direct their own processes and tool usage."

Reasonix is solidly in the workflow camp. The path is fixed: read REASONIX.md, read STYLE_GUIDE.md, read the existing text, diagnose, edit at smallest scope, self-check, return. The subagent never decides which rules to apply or in what order. It never chooses between drafting and editing based on context. It follows the script.

Anthropic's team observed a consistent pattern among successful agent deployments: "the most successful implementations used simple, composable patterns rather than complex frameworks." Reasonix is about as simple as it gets. A few Markdown files. A set of rules. A fixed workflow. No framework, no orchestration layer, no dynamic task decomposition.

The simplicity is the point. Every layer of abstraction between the instruction and the output introduces a new surface for misinterpretation. A rule like "no em dashes" is trivially checkable by a human and trivially enforceable by an LLM. A rule like "be funny but dry" is neither. The more you wrap these instructions in frameworks that claim to "understand" voice, the more you lose the ability to debug when the output goes wrong.

This is also why the system can publish without review. The safety rules are hard constraints, not suggestions. Rule 5: never delete an existing post. Rule 4: never modify layout or component files without asking. The publish command runs a build first and aborts on failure. These are not probabilistic guardrails. They are binary gates.

## What This Is Not

Reasonix is not a general-purpose writing tool. It is hyper-specific to one blog, one voice, one set of obsessions. The style guide knows about Shrek's onions and Vygotsky and the difference between the 2025 and 2026 registers. It knows that TPACK should never be defined inline. It knows that "at the end of the day" is banned and "utilize" is a crime.

None of this transfers to another writer. If you dropped STYLE_GUIDE.md into a different blog by a different person, it would produce dissonant, constrained, rule-following prose that sounds like someone doing an impression of Jeremy Sheeshka from memory. The voice is not portable. The constraints that produce it are not general writing advice. They are specific reactions to specific tendencies in my own writing and in the default output patterns of LLMs.

This is maybe the most important thing I can say about the experiment. The value is not in the template. The value is in the specificity. I wrote those 20 rules by noticing what I kept having to fix in AI-generated drafts. Rule 4 (no hedging) exists because LLMs hedge constantly. Rule 10 (no em dashes) exists because LLMs love em dashes with an unsettling passion. Rule 19 (no corporate vocabulary) exists because "utilize" and "facilitate" and "leverage" are the default vocabulary of every language model trained on business prose.

The style guide is not a theory of good writing. It is a record of repairs.

## Where This Goes Next

I do not know if this approach generalizes. I suspect it does not. I suspect the only way to make an AI write in your voice is to do what I did: spend months noticing the gap between what you meant and what the AI produced, then encode every single gap as a rule, then test the rules, then notice new gaps, then encode those too. It is an iterative process, not a one-time configuration.

The evaluator-optimizer pattern from the Anthropic taxonomy describes this precisely: "one LLM call generates a response while another provides evaluation and feedback in a loop." In Reasonix, the evaluation step is split between the subagent's self-check (mechanical, checklist-driven) and my own review (judgment-driven). The loop runs as many times as it takes. Some posts go through one pass. Some go through five.

But the loop has an endpoint that the agent cannot reach on its own. Only I know when the post sounds like me. The agent can enforce the rules. It cannot feel the voice. That boundary, the one between rule-following and voice-recognition, is where Reasonix stops being useful and starts being dangerous. Use it past that point and you get posts that check every box and sound like nobody.
