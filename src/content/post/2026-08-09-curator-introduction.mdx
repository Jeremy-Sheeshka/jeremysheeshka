Institutions sit on knowledge they can't safely hand to cloud AI: lesson recordings, student records, parent notes, billing information. In BC, privacy considerations make it a real liability to hand student or client data to a public cloud AI. No major AI product was built with that liability in mind, or with a way to integrate into the offline tech stacks these institutions already run.

<div class="clear-both"></div>


## Curator: The Local AI Infrastructure 

Curator is open-source, local-first AI infrastructure that runs on hardware a school or studio already owns, so that knowledge never has to leave the building to be useful.

Rather than sending data to the cloud for processing, Curator brings the AI to the data. It is designed to be deployed on the existing hardware a school or studio already has, keeping sensitive information like student records, lesson content, and billing data within the institution's physical control.

This is not a cloud product with a privacy policy that promises not to look at your data. It is infrastructure that makes it structurally impossible for your data to leave the building unless you explicitly choose to share it.

## Cadenza: The Flagship Application

Cadenza, our first application of this technology, shows what that looks like in practice: it listens to a lesson locally, drafts a summary, retrieves and transposes the right sheet music, answers voice questions against your own uploaded documents, and shares curated information with clients securely.

For example, you might ask it to find the Clarinet 3 part in a concert band arrangement and email it to two of your students. It's that easy. No digging through a filing cabinet. No drafting any emails. No trip to the photocopier!

Cadenza is our flagship application and technological wedge, which we are bringing to market, of Curator. It demonstrates that local-first AI is not a compromise or a stepping stone toward something cloud-based. It is the destination: a model where institutions own their data, own their infrastructure, and still get the full power of AI.

<span class="text-lg">Strengths/Weaknesses of the Pitch</span>

<div class="mt-4 space-y-4 text-sm text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed">
<p>Starting out on my journey through ETEC 522, I think I strongly identified as an intrapreneur, someone comfortable finding creative solutions and making small improvements within existing boundaries and capabilities. Taking on the role of an EVA in Assignment 1 forced me to check in to reality and truly consider the context and complexities of how ideas get tested by markets and investors: market durability, future growth potential, the existing competition landscape, credibility, and what a venture pitch is actually made of. The strength of my pitch is that its core is lived rather than researched: the wedge problem is a Tuesday I have actually had, and the regulatory tailwind is real law, not a trend I invented. The weakness is that past that wedge, the pitch remains broad and speculative: it leans on institutional unknowns I have not yet validated, like partnerships, privacy review, and district adoption, and despite the reduced barriers to entry through free, open distribution, the rate of growth may be unpredictable or non-existent altogether.</p>

<p>If I'm honest about where my inclinations land now, they sit somewhere between the intrapreneur I started out as and the champion founder this assignment asked me to become. I leaned toward this venture for three reasons: it serves a purpose I believe in (privacy tooling and data sovereignty), it sits in the overlap of what I actually know (music and technology), and it matches my instinctively human-first way of working, proving one idea before moving to the next. Named plainly, that inclination leans toward a lifestyle venture rather than scale-at-all-costs, and I can see it inside the strategy itself: land-prove-expand is just the intrapreneur's discipline (start small, show it works, then ask for more) applied at a founder's scale.</p>

<span class="text-lg">AI Reflections</span>

<div class="mt-4 space-y-4 text-sm text-gray-700 dark:text-gray-300 leading-relaxed">
<p>In developing and reflecting on my dual-pronged pitch for Curator and its flagship application, Cadenza, I have mixed thoughts to share. I began brainstorming with tools like Claude and Qwen as sounding boards, only to find myself pulled in so many directions at once and suffering from scope creep. Through that, I became determined enough in my choice of venture that I had to build a minimum viable prototype of my own to try these ideas against reality. What began as one idea grew to encompass two layers with different market horizons: the present, what I could actually implement and validate now, and the future the venture might grow into if it became investment-worthy. I had an entrepreneurial inclination to not stop until I found a remix of existing tools that could stand on its own with meaningful potential in the real world.</p>

<p>I spent part of this process chasing idealistic blue-ocean ideas, trying to determine how my own background and knowledge could be implemented here and now, but ultimately the test became whether I felt comfortable standing behind the pitch and defending it to a skeptic. I chose this endeavour because no competitor is offering what I had in mind: a suite of vertical tech stacks built around offline, local-first solutions to administrative workflows. That said, I found that while the AI was effective at refining and aligning ideas to a business mindset, it lacked the lay of the land and the real-world contextual edge to meaningfully apply what it was pitching. The tools kept offering me bigger asks, richer ratios, and bolder compliance claims than I could defend, and I had to be the one to pull every number back to something honest. They were excellent at structure and terrible at restraint; the judgment had to be mine.</p>

<p>In keeping with the responsible, ethical, and transparent use of artificial intelligence, generative AI tools were used throughout the ideation, writing, visual design, and iterative development of this venture pitch. AI-generated content and suggestions were critically reviewed, revised, and approved by the author before publication.</p>
