### Exercise 1.3:

## List five areas of your life that could be games. Then briefly describe a possible underlying game structure for each.

<Steps>
  <Step title="House Renovations">
    I had a leak inside of my front wall that I had to find recently. Problem solving often makes for a great game strucutre when there is an an application of rules or criteria to a process. For fixing my wall, I had to chase the leak from my basement, through my main floor, to my upstairs bedroom window. For me, the name of the game was to open up the wall enough to get everything dry without taking off too much drywall so neatness and forethought becomes part of my own gamification of the task. The underlying game structure I would apply to this is rule setting and creation as if the player was the game designer themselves.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Teaching Grade Two">
    Engaging students in literacy or numeracy activities in my Grade 2 class can be tricky! Often times I find myself thinking of ways to engage students by way of playful challenge in my class with different activities. The other week I introduced my class to the game BINGO. Having played a few games now with numbers, and letters, I changed things up today by calling out math problems instead (ie. "The next square is 2 + 3 ).  Three thoughts I have towards the underlying game strucutre is in how "everyone has a turn at once" during each round (so the mechanics of the gameplay), having each students place their own numbers (create their own card), and also how BINGO in and of itself can be used as a platform for a variety of questions and content.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Playing Jazz Guitar">
    Imposing rules and criteria over chord changes. "This time I'm just going to play arpeggio's over the chords" or "I'm going to play every dominant seven chord with a sharp five." The underlying game structure that I think about here is towards how the players are coordinating themselves towards or around something. In way this is part the self imposition of rules again, but also playing with and coordinating themselves amongst others by way of sight or sound.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Going Grocery Shopping">
    The process of crunching numbers at the grocery store trying to guess how much something is, if something is a good deal or not per the quantity, or simply, seeing how fast you can make it in and out during rush hour, all could be games. Game structures at play here would be time, money, prices (math and real-world problem solving skills), along with an unpredictable element of people and an awareness of time and oneself.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Cooking">
    Making my lunch during lunchtime, I mean business. The name of the game here for me would be optimizing the process so that everything gets made quickly and efficiently so I can have time to enjoy eating my lunch. An underlying game structure related to this would Be self-challenge and optimization.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<div className="sparkle-wrapper">
  <Callout variant="info" title="My thoughts">
      Through the above, I would say that some of the trends I have found myself thinking about are the "playcentric" elements that are often superimposed onto the games including challenges, patterns, or opportunities for meaning-making, optimization of process or a narrative.
  </Callout>
</div>

### Exercise 2.6:

## Name three games that you find particularly challenging and describe why.

<div className="challenge-section">
  <p className="intro-text">Out of games I have played, I would say that I find the following to be challenging for different reasons:</p>
  
  <div className="challenge-card">
    <h4 className="challenge-title">Game of Risk</h4>
    <p className="challenge-description">
      With Risk often comes challenges between players as they form alliances and oppositions against one another over territory. Given the high-stakes nature of the game, in the times I have played it, there always seem to be negotiations and shifting relationships that emerge between players. These dynamics often create tension and strong emotions as decisions are made across multiple rounds, especially when strategic choices intersect with the chance-based elements of the game.
    </p>
  </div>
  
  <div className="challenge-card">
    <h4 className="challenge-title">Trivial Pursuit</h4>
    <p className="challenge-description">
      I am not particularly knowledgeable about pop culture or many of the categories that frequently appear in the game. Being required to recall specific facts that I may or may not know can be challenging, and this sometimes influences my motivation and engagement during gameplay. The game highlights how knowledge itself can become a source of pressure, where knowing—or not knowing—shapes one's experience of challenge and participation.
    </p>
  </div>
  
  <div className="challenge-card">
    <h4 className="challenge-title">Boggle</h4>
    <p className="challenge-description">
      Over the holidays, I was playing Boggle with a few friends on my TV, and what I found most challenging was actually stopping myself from playing it. Because the gameplay was visible to everyone on the screen, it became difficult to withhold informal participation, even when it wasn't technically my turn. The game felt fast-paced, familiar, simple, and addictive, and its flat mechanics gradually turned into a cognitive challenge as I felt compelled to constantly search for patterns and words. In this way, Boggle became less about competition and more about the tension between observation, impulse, and self-control.
    </p>
  </div>
</div>
