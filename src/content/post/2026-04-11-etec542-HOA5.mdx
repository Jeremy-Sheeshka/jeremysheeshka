## 1. Setting the Scene

I teach Grade Two in Prince Rupert, BC, in a school where the majority of my students are First Nations. I have played guitar most of my life and have held various music education roles across elementary and secondary schools, and the question I keep returning to is whether I should be engaging with First Nations music and storytelling at all, and if so, whether I could ever do that authentically as a non-Indigenous person trained in another musical tradition myself?

To give this some context, before my time in Prince Rupert, I had worked for a few years in villages north of Terrace (Laxgalts'ap, Gitwinksihlkw, and Gingolx) and played in the Majagaleehl Nisga'a community band on Nisga'a Territory during my time there. In this previous teaching role, a longstanding community band member gave me permission to use a First Nations melody with my beginner band class and provided a notated copy of the melody. I took this and arranged it for my elementary concert band students and then had them play it as part of their repertoire. All I had been told about this melody was that it was a spring song. Looking back, I am still not sure what to make of this experience. While I had permission to do this, I didn't have much context about it at the time. I was able to take something small that had been shared with me and make it into something I could then share with my students. Given that I was living in this village at the time, was this a responsible inclusion or just a more well-intentioned version of indigenous decorated music education?

That moment prompts this question for me: as a music educator working predominantly with First Nations students, I want to know what genuine place-based inclusion actually looks like as a non-indigenous educator. Is this something I can actually approach with honestly from my own musical training within the limits of what I actually have standing to do?

---

## 2. Connecting to Course Learning

My question about whether I was decorating a Western model with Indigenous elements connects directly to some of the content and discussions I encountered in this course. Looking back at my arrangement of the Nisga'a spring song, I can now see that even with permission and good intentions, I may have been imposing Indigenous knowledge into an essentialist framework of Western music education.

<Cite n={1} title="Rogers (2006)" /> points out that cultural appropriation is complex, and that the political implications of borrowing cultural artifacts are not entirely determined by the intentions of the person doing the borrowing. This was a point that clicked for me. Even with active permission, I had borrowed this cultural material and translated it into a format that made sense by my Western standards for my band students.

Connecting this instance further to the course through our learning about Hawaiian pedagogy of aloha, where teaching is rooted in kuleana, mutual trust, and community, <Cite n={2} title="Gunawardena et al. (2019)" /> helped me to rationalize and let go of the idea that I needed to be an authority on traditions that are not mine.

---

## 3. Describing My Application

In my reflection of this, the spring song arrangement is actually an interesting benchmark for how I might approach indigenous music education content differently in the future. I don't think the problem was the melody itself necessarily or that I had used it with permission, perhaps what I could have done differently would be to be more mindful about the context and background I had shared along aside it when I taught it. In addition to that, having arranged this music myself after only being given a written melody, perhaps I should have reapproached the community member that shared it with me and sought their advice on what I come up with as if to understand the cultural framework behind it and if there was a time or way to teach or share it.

Alongside that point, perhaps to engage students further with music learning, I could have taught the music for this by ear through my voice, rather than notation, or through call and response to engage watching and imitating rather than through reading.

Another thought about this, would have been to invite the community member themselves into my class as if to have this act of music learning become more sharedly collaborative and involved from them. I could have created the community-based co-design conditions where the knowledge keeper themselves were actually present in the room given our friendship had I thought about that at the time.

---

## 4. Reflecting on Significance and Future Directions

One of the biggest shifts for me through reflecting on this experience and my question of how I could genuinely include indigenized music content into my own teaching practice now, has been realizing that things aren't always about permission. While I had that, what I didn't necessarily have was a full understanding of context, collaboration, or any real understanding of the cultural framework behind it on my part. That's what I'd like to keep working on in the future within my teaching practice.

That said, I know I’ll still be reflecting on how to build genuine relationships with knowledge keepers in my community, so I can create opportunities to engage more deeply with content outside my cultural comfort zone. I suppose that my next step would be to open up further place-based conversations with the network of people I have in my community, and explore the wisdom that remains around me hidden in plain sight.
