I have been experimenting with something quietly for months. It sits in a directory called `.reasonix/` at the root of this blog's repo. Most readers will never see it. It is not a product. It is not a startup. It is a set of Markdown files that tell an AI how to write like me.

The experiment started with a question I could not shake: can you encode a distinctive writing voice into machine-readable instructions well enough that an AI can draft, edit, and even publish in that voice without flattening it?

I did not expect the answer to be yes. And it is not. Not fully. But the partial yes is more interesting than any full no I had assumed going in.

The system is called Reasonix. This post is about what it actually is, what it can actually do, and where it breaks down. No hype. No "AI is the future of writing" hand-waving. Just the real setup and the real problems.

## What Reasonix Actually Is

Reasonix is not an application. It is a directory of agent definition files that an AI coding assistant reads before doing anything on this blog.

The directory structure looks like this:

```
REASONIX.md                          # shared context, hard safety rules
.reasonix/
  skills/
    blog-writer/
      SKILL.md                       # the writing subagent definition
      STYLE_GUIDE.md                 # 20 voice rules, 4 voice traits
    astro-dev/
      SKILL.md                       # the dev subagent
    frontmatter/
      SKILL.md                       # frontmatter generator
    media/
      SKILL.md                       # image/media handler
  commands/
    new-post.md                      # scaffold a draft
    publish.md                       # build, commit, push
```

Each file is a system-level instruction set. When I invoke the writing subagent, it reads `REASONIX.md` first for shared context, then `STYLE_GUIDE.md` for the voice rules, then its own `SKILL.md` for role-specific behavior. Only then does it touch a word.

The rules are not vague. Rule 10: no em dashes. Rule 4: cut hedging phrases like "I feel that" and "I believe that." Rule 15: every citation carries the author's take immediately after it. Rule 3: do not define jargon the author left undefined. The style guide runs to 180 lines with a quick-reference banned-phrase list, before/after examples, and a conflict resolution order.

This level of specificity is not an aesthetic choice. It is load-bearing architecture. Without it, the system defaults to generic AI-written prose. With it, something closer to my actual voice emerges.

The prompt engineering literature has a term for this kind of layered instruction: Schulhoff et al. (2024) catalogued 58 distinct prompting techniques in their systematic survey and found that the most effective strategies combine multiple techniques into highly specific instruction sets rather than relying on any single one. <Cite n={1} title="The Prompt Report" /> Reasonix is not one prompt. It is a stack of them. Each constrains a different dimension of the output.

## What It Can Actually Do

Four things. Each is real, but each comes with a catch.

**Draft in voice.** Give the writing subagent an outline or a set of notes and it will produce a draft that reads like me. Not perfectly. But close enough that editing takes minutes instead of hours. The agent applies all 20 rules, checks for the four named moves (THINKING-ALOUD, COMMIT-BIT, JARGON-TRUST, CITE-THEN-TAKE), and defaults to the tighter 2026 register. It then runs a self-check before returning anything.

**Edit against voice rules.** Feed it an existing paragraph that has drifted into academic hedging or corporate filler and the subagent will diagnose the specific violations by rule number, then rewrite at the smallest possible scope. It will not "improve" the paragraph. It will fix rule 4, or rule 18, or rule 19, and leave everything else alone. This is the hardest discipline to enforce in AI-assisted writing, and it is the one that matters most. The subagent's mandate reads: "Do not rewrite a paragraph that only needs one sentence cut."

**Scaffold posts.** The `new-post` command reads 3-5 existing posts to match frontmatter conventions, generates a kebab-case slug, creates the `.mdx` file with `draft: true`, writes a description under 160 characters, suggests tags reusing the existing vocabulary, and creates the image directory. Then it stops and tells me what to do next. It does not write body text. It does not guess what the post is about.

**Build, commit, push.** The `publish` command runs `pnpm build` first. If the build fails, it stops cold and reports the errors. On a green build, it stages everything, generates a commit message from the changed files (format: `post: <slug>: <one-line summary>`), commits, and pushes. Netlify auto-deploys on push. The agent then tells me what was committed, what the message was, confirms the push succeeded, and reminds me how to revert. Rule 3 from REASONIX.md: "Agents may publish or push a post live without waiting on review first, but must always inform the author immediately when they do: what changed, where it's live, and how to revert it."

All of this works. Not flawlessly. But it works often enough to be useful. The key insight is that none of these capabilities is novel on its own. Every AI writing tool on the market can draft a blog post. What makes Reasonix different is that it does not stop at drafting. It layers drafting, editing, scaffolding, and publishing into a single workflow where each step is constrained by the same voice rules, the same safety rules, and the same verification steps.

## The Constraint Paradox

Here is where it gets interesting.

The more rules I add to STYLE_GUIDE.md, the more consistent the output becomes. Rule 10 (no em dashes) is trivially enforceable. Rule 4 (no hedging) catches 90% of what makes AI prose sound like AI prose. Rule 19 (no corporate vocabulary: "utilize" becomes "use," "facilitate" becomes "help") eliminates an entire register that most LLMs default to.

But more rules also create new failure modes. The subagent sometimes over-applies a rule in a context where it does not belong. A sentence that genuinely needs a long breath gets chopped into fragments because rule 8 says sentences should mostly stay under 25 words. A paragraph that actually benefits from a transitional phrase gets stripped because rule 18 says no filler transitions. The rules are blunt instruments. They do not understand nuance. They apply themselves mechanically.

This is the constraint paradox. Patricia Stokes spent a career studying it. In *Creativity from Constraints: The Psychology of Breakthrough*, she argued that constraints do not merely limit creative output. They structure the problem space. <Cite n={2} title="Creativity from Constraints" /> A painter who restricts herself to a single brush makes different decisions than one with a full studio. A sonnet's fourteen lines do not inhibit the poet; they give the poet something to push against.

The same dynamic operates in Reasonix, but with a twist. The constraints are not self-imposed by the writer. They are imposed by a second system that does not understand *why* a given constraint exists. The style guide knows that em dashes are banned, but not why I banned them. It knows that COMMIT-BIT paragraphs must not be undercut, but it does not know when something is or is not a COMMIT-BIT. It pattern-matches. Sometimes it matches wrong.

What I have learned is that the voice rules work best as a backstop, not as a generative engine. When I draft a paragraph myself and ask the subagent to tighten it, the result is almost always better. The rules catch what I miss. When I ask the subagent to draft from scratch, the result is usable but requires heavier editing. The rules cannot supply what they are designed to protect. They can only protect what is already there.

## Where It Breaks Down

Five failure modes. Each one tells you something about what these systems are actually doing under the hood.

**Failure 1: It cannot originate an idea.** Reasonix can draft a post from notes. It cannot decide what is worth writing about. The subagent has no access to my experiences, my classroom, my students, or my internal sense of what is interesting. It can work with what I give it. It cannot generate what I have not yet noticed. This is the largest gap, and it is structural. No amount of voice rules will close it.

**Failure 2: It cannot verify a fact.** REASONIX.md rule 1 says: "Never invent a fact, statistic, citation, or quote." The subagent follows this. But following it means it cannot independently check whether something I wrote is accurate. If I claim something false in my notes, the agent will draft it into the post without objection. It is a style enforcer, not a fact-checker.

**Failure 3: It over-applies rules in edge cases.** The self-check at the end of every draft is a checklist. Checklists do not exercise judgment. A sentence that needs to be 30 words for rhythm will get flagged by rule 8. A rhetorical move that looks like hedging but is actually a deliberate gesture of uncertainty gets flattened into a declarative. The subagent's conflict resolution clause says clarity beats voice, but it cannot reliably tell when clarity has been served and when it has been sacrificed.

**Failure 4: It cannot sustain a COMMIT-BIT from scratch.** The COMMIT-BIT is the hardest move to automate. It requires setting up something slightly absurd and treating it with total seriousness across multiple paragraphs without ever winking at the reader. The subagent can preserve an existing COMMIT-BIT. It can edit one without undercutting it. But generating one cold, with the right premise, the right pacing, and the right restraint, almost never lands. The humor comes out forced or the commitment wobbles. This is not a prompt engineering problem. It is a fundamental limitation of a system that has never actually found anything funny.

**Failure 5: The system drifts when it is not re-grounded.** Each invocation of the subagent reads STYLE_GUIDE.md fresh. In theory, this means it never forgets the rules. In practice, long drafting sessions across multiple invocations produce subtle drift. A rule gets applied slightly differently. A register choice shifts from tighter to looser. The subagent does not have memory of its own previous decisions, so it cannot calibrate against them. The consistency is invocation-level, not session-level.

## The Architecture That Makes It Work

Under the hood, Reasonix follows what Anthropic (2024) calls a workflow pattern, as distinct from a fully autonomous agent. <Cite n={3} title="Building effective agents" /> Workflows are "systems where LLMs and tools are orchestrated through predefined code paths." Agents are "systems where LLMs dynamically direct their own processes and tool usage."

Reasonix is solidly in the workflow camp. The path is fixed: read REASONIX.md, read STYLE_GUIDE.md, read the existing text, diagnose, edit at smallest scope, self-check, return. The subagent never decides which rules to apply or in what order. It never chooses between drafting and editing based on context. It follows the script.

Anthropic's team observed a consistent pattern among successful agent deployments: "the most successful implementations used simple, composable patterns rather than complex frameworks." Reasonix is about as simple as it gets. A few Markdown files. A set of rules. A fixed workflow. No framework, no orchestration layer, no dynamic task decomposition.

The simplicity is the point. Every layer of abstraction between the instruction and the output introduces a new surface for misinterpretation. A rule like "no em dashes" is trivially checkable by a human and trivially enforceable by an LLM. A rule like "be funny but dry" is neither. The more you wrap these instructions in frameworks that claim to "understand" voice, the more you lose the ability to debug when the output goes wrong.

This is also why the system can publish without review. The safety rules are hard constraints, not suggestions. Rule 5: never delete an existing post. Rule 4: never modify layout or component files without asking. The publish command runs a build first and aborts on failure. These are not probabilistic guardrails. They are binary gates.

## What This Is Not

Reasonix is not a general-purpose writing tool. It is hyper-specific to one blog, one voice, one set of obsessions. The style guide knows about Shrek's onions and Vygotsky and the difference between the 2025 and 2026 registers. It knows that TPACK should never be defined inline. It knows that "at the end of the day" is banned and "utilize" is a crime.

None of this transfers to another writer. If you dropped STYLE_GUIDE.md into a different blog by a different person, it would produce dissonant, constrained, rule-following prose that sounds like someone doing an impression of Jeremy Sheeshka from memory. The voice is not portable. The constraints that produce it are not general writing advice. They are specific reactions to specific tendencies in my own writing and in the default output patterns of LLMs.

This is maybe the most important thing I can say about the experiment. The value is not in the template. The value is in the specificity. I wrote those 20 rules by noticing what I kept having to fix in AI-generated drafts. Rule 4 (no hedging) exists because LLMs hedge constantly. Rule 10 (no em dashes) exists because LLMs love em dashes with an unsettling passion. Rule 19 (no corporate vocabulary) exists because "utilize" and "facilitate" and "leverage" are the default vocabulary of every language model trained on business prose.

The style guide is not a theory of good writing. It is a record of repairs.

## Where This Goes Next

I do not know if this approach generalizes. I suspect it does not. I suspect the only way to make an AI write in your voice is to do what I did: spend months noticing the gap between what you meant and what the AI produced, then encode every single gap as a rule, then test the rules, then notice new gaps, then encode those too. It is an iterative process, not a one-time configuration.

The evaluator-optimizer pattern from the Anthropic taxonomy describes this precisely: "one LLM call generates a response while another provides evaluation and feedback in a loop." In Reasonix, the evaluation step is split between the subagent's self-check (mechanical, checklist-driven) and my own review (judgment-driven). The loop runs as many times as it takes. Some posts go through one pass. Some go through five.

But the loop has an endpoint that the agent cannot reach on its own. Only I know when the post sounds like me. The agent can enforce the rules. It cannot feel the voice. That boundary, the one between rule-following and voice-recognition, is where Reasonix stops being useful and starts being dangerous. Use it past that point and you get posts that check every box and sound like nobody.
