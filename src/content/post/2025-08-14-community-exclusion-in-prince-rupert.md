<div class="fake-h2">Dedicated to everyone who has been quietly left out of community arts in Prince Rupert</div>

*This post reflects my personal experiences and views as a member of the Prince Rupert arts community.*

<br>

Community spaces are meant to be inclusive by design. They are supposed to be places where participation emerges naturally, where people come together through shared interest, and where the non-neutrality of social and organizational structures is acknowledged and addressed. This is why my experience in the Prince Rupert arts community has remained difficult to ignore. It highlights how participation can be quietly shaped, limited, or redirected by those who hold influence, and how easily exclusion can become normalized when left unchallenged.

An example of this came around 2018–2019, when Michael Gurney, then general manager of the Lester Centre of the Arts, informed me over the phone that I was unable to participate in the community musical *Disaster!*. Kristy Tillman, who had volunteered as part of the production team, had explicitly requested that I be excluded from participating. I was in disbelief. As I saw it, here was someone using their volunteer role and social position within the arts community to leverage who could be involved in a community event, and on what terms. It was difficult not to see this as an exercise of power rather than an act rooted in community or leadership.

Unfortunately, this situation has not been isolated. It has repeated over the years and reflects a broader pattern within the local arts scene here in Prince Rupert. I want to call this out for what I believe it is: a pattern of exclusion that our community organizations have allowed to continue. It shows how community spaces that are assumed to be open and welcoming can become shaped by behind-the-scenes determinations of who belongs, who is heard, and who is quietly pushed out.

In 2018, this exclusion extended to the Special Events Society as well. Miranda Kessler, an organizer with the society, told me that I was not able to participate in the scheduling of the community's music events because the same person flat out refused to collaborate with me.

Another example of this stands out to me, where that same person volunteered to conduct the Prince Rupert Community Band. The group was advertised publicly as open to everyone, and yet when I attended rehearsal in October 2023, the rehearsal was suddenly cancelled and they stepped down, refusing to conduct the ensemble with me in attendance. As John Turner put it to me: "If you're in, she's out." On November 8, 2023, the band announced that all practices were suspended for the foreseeable future. This decision did not only impact me. It affected every participant who simply wanted to engage in music making.

I later discovered that Dawn Quast had silently removed my name from the Prince Rupert music community's mailing list, effectively excluding me again without any conversation or accountability. The irony is difficult to ignore here, as I had previously conducted the community band and contributed to it for years as a trombone player alongside the very person who now refused to conduct it with me there.

This matters beyond my own experience because of how much of Prince Rupert's music community runs through one person. Kristy Tillman sits on the board of the Lester Centre of the Arts, organizes music events through the Special Events Society, and has a hand in deciding who participates in the community musical through its audition process. When the same person holds a seat on the venue's board, organizes the community's music events, and has a say in who is cast, there is no independent check on who gets to take part. In my view, this is a conflict of interest, and it is what allows exclusion like mine to happen quietly and without accountability.

I have tried to resolve this directly, but Kristy Tillman has refused to mediate with me or to communicate with me in any capacity about these exclusions.

I would like to see the Lester Centre and the Special Events Society adopt clear conflict-of-interest rules for board members who also run programs, use independent panels for auditions, and make sure that groups advertised as open to everyone actually are.

These experiences have led me to want to share this exclusion publicly to acknowledge it for what it is. Community exclusion in community music is not just a personal issue; it undermines the ethical foundation of what community arts are supposed to be. Sadly, when community members tolerate or overlook this kind of behavior, they contribute to a culture where participation becomes conditional, where power goes unchecked, and where exclusion becomes routine rather than questioned.

To create genuine community, we must recognize that participation is shaped by the systems and people who structure these spaces. We must ask whose voices and presence are valued, whose are dismissed, and why. I believe that there is simply no place for exclusionary practices in any community arts environment. The moment we allow them, we compromise the very idea of community itself.

While this post is drawn from personal experience, its ideas connect with broader conversations about the participatory cultures educators establish and contribute to, and the non-neutrality of learning environments which are often ignored beyond the classroom.
