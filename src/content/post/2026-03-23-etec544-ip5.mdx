## Rationale
 
For this intellectual production, I examined the ways video game culture systematically excludes women and other marginalized groups through layered structures of harassment, gatekeeping, and cultural expectation.

## What Is Hegemonic Game Culture?
 
At its core, video game culture is built around (and continues to serve) a narrow demographic: cisgendered, white, male players. Technical mastery in gameplay has become a proxy for masculine identity, meaning that to be good at games is, culturally, to be male. This isn't incidental. It's structural.
 
This hegemony plays out across three interconnected arenas:

## The Boundaries: How They're Built and Kept
 
### In Competitive Play
 
The case of Geguri — a highly skilled South Korean *Overwatch* professional — makes these dynamics impossible to ignore. When she outperformed her male peers, the community's response wasn't admiration: it was accusations of cheating. <Cite n={2} title="Choi, Slaker & Ahmad (2020)" /> Even after a live demonstration cleared her name, the attacks shifted to her appearance, her age, her reluctance to be called a "feminist icon." Skill wasn't enough. She also had to justify her right to exist in the space.
 
This is the pattern. Women in esports don't simply compete — they are surveilled, questioned, and required to perform their legitimacy in ways men never are. <Cite n={6} title="Witkowski (2018)" /> A recent empirical study by Crothers, Scott-Brown, and Cunningham (2024) confirms that these experiences are not isolated: women in esports report pervasive harassment that ranges from verbal abuse to doxing and sexual threats, with many eventually leaving competitive spaces altogether. <Cite n={7} title="Crothers, Scott-Brown & Cunningham (2024)" />

### In the Industry Itself
 
The problems don't stop at the player level. The workplace culture at studios like Ubisoft Toronto has been described as a protected ecosystem of harm — where powerful men faced no accountability, HR structures were compromised by personal relationships, and women who spoke up risked their careers. <Cite n={5} title="Schreier (2020)" /> The message sent to women considering a career in games is clear: you are not safe here.
 
### Through Tropes and Representation
 
Within games themselves, women are frequently flattened into stereotypes: the healer, the love interest, the reward. The "girl gamer" label strips women of individual identity and assigns them a corner of the culture they're permitted to occupy — as long as they remain palatable to male audiences. Refusing that corner invites hostility.

## Data Reliability & Positionality
 
It's worth acknowledging that much of the data on harassment in gaming is self-reported or anecdotal, which means it likely *undercounts* the real scope of the problem. Marginalized players who have already been pushed out of the space aren't surveyed. Historical mistrust of institutions — whether that's a game studio's HR department or a tournament organizer — may discourage reporting entirely.
 
I also bring my own positionality to this topic: I am a man writing about the experiences of women and LGBTQ+ people in gaming. I've tried to center the voices from the research, but this is a perspective I hold with care. What I can offer is an examination of the structures that enable harm — and a commitment to using my own access to gaming and educational spaces to disrupt those structures. I cannot speak to the lived experience of being harassed in a voice chat, but I can refuse to be silent when I witness it.

## Findings
 
One of the clearest takeaways from this research is that the barriers aren't loud and singular — they're quiet and cumulative. A woman doesn't leave gaming because of one comment. She leaves because of thousands of comments, structural invisibility, and the constant expenditure of energy proving she belongs.
 
All-female esports leagues represent one proposed solution, offering safer developmental spaces and removing the burden of representing all women in a single performance. <Cite n={6} title="Witkowski (2018)" /> But segregated leagues also risk leaving the men's side of the culture entirely intact — and unexamined.
 
The more uncomfortable truth is that changing game culture requires changing the men in it.

## How Boundaries Change (or Don't)
 
Efforts to shift hegemonic game culture operate at multiple levels:
 
- **Grassroots resistance:** Organizations like *Women in Games*, *AnyKey*, and community-led initiatives provide mentorship, safe spaces, and advocacy. These groups challenge the idea that gaming is inherently male and create pathways for marginalized players to enter and stay.
- **Institutional reforms:** Some game companies now employ dedicated moderation teams, deploy AI tools to detect harassment, and publish transparency reports. Esports leagues have introduced anti-harassment policies and, in a few cases, diversity quotas. <Cite n={8} title="Rogstad (2022)" />
- **Persistent barriers:** Despite these efforts, Rogstad's (2022) literature review reveals that the underlying structures of esports remain overwhelmingly male-dominated. Media coverage still frames women players as novelties, and the burden of proving legitimacy continues to fall on individuals rather than systems.
 
Thus, while boundaries *can* shift, they often do so slowly and incompletely. The default culture is resilient because it is embedded not only in policies but in everyday interactions, media narratives, and the design of games themselves.

## Implications for Teaching Practice
 
This is where my role as a K-12 educator becomes concrete. The classroom is where the next generation of players, developers, and community members is being shaped.
 
<Callout title="Burning Question" variant="note">
  How do I actively model inclusive gaming culture in my classroom — not just by adding representation, but by disrupting the underlying assumption that STEM and gaming spaces belong to some students more than others?
</Callout>
 
Girls were twice as likely to draw a boy when asked to draw a scientist or mathematician. Boys drew boys almost universally. <Cite n={4} title="Edutopia (2019)" /> If gaming spaces replicate this, and they do, then the K-12 classroom is both a site of the problem and one of the most powerful places to address it.
 
One strategy I've begun using is a **cooperative game session** where students are randomly assigned roles that defy gender stereotypes — for example, in *Overcooked*, everyone rotates between "support" and "lead" positions, and we debrief afterward on who assumed authority and why. Another practice is **explicit language intervention**: when a student uses a phrase like "you play like a girl," we pause to analyze what that phrase actually means and whose work it does.
 
Teachers who model inclusivity will see students who practice it — in classrooms, in lobbies, and eventually, in studios.

## Reflections
 
Before engaging seriously with this research, I understood harassment in gaming as something *other* people did. What shifted for me was recognizing the structural nature of it — that the problem isn't a few bad actors, but a culture that consistently produces and protects them.
 
The lack of opportunity for women to engage in this industry is not only a concern for gender equality in gaming — it is an economic and creative failure. The games being made are narrower, less imaginative, and less human than they could be.
 
My responsibility isn't just to raise boys who treat people well online. It's to create classrooms where girls don't have to fight for the right to belong in digital spaces before they've even started.
