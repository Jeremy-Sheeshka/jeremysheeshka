For my Week 8 Doings post, I took a lead sheet of a jazz standard and shifted its modality into an interactive music-education/art game. <i>Cotton Tail</i> was what I ended up with. It is a game where the player (Bunny Webster) follows, imitates, and trades movement phrases with a mentor rabbit (The Duke). As a nod to the great Jazz Musicians Duke Ellington and Ben Webster, <i>Cotton Tail</i> serves as a precursor to jazz education, through an interactive entanglement of jazz history and aural pedagogy. 

The game is built with Phaser and the Web Audio API, and it's designed to have the user experience call-and-response, improvisation, AABA form, and observational skills through spatial gameplay rather than music notation or other written literacies. Together, two rabbits take the player on a natural journey progressing through a cycle of learning, following, imitating, internalizing, experimenting, and creating.

The idea behind translating this song into another modality began as a pun, but it grew into a chance to examine how gamification can support "musicking" (simultaneously celebrating, listening to, and producing music) alongside other media. By inviting the user to follow, imitate, and eventually carve their own path through the game, I have been able to represent the essence of what it means to teach, learn, and engage with music concepts through the transmutation of spatial improvisation.

## Reflection

<div class="md:float-left md:w-1/4 md:mr-6 md:mb-4 w-3/4 sm:w-1/2 mx-auto md:mx-0 mb-6">
  <Figure
    src="/assets/images/blog/cottontail-sheet.png"
    alt="Lead sheet excerpt of Duke Ellington's Cotton Tail"
  />
</div>

**What did the original mode assume about who could access it?** Engaging with "Cotton Tail" in its original form assumes a player proficient enough on an instrument to navigate the chord progression, in print or by ear. That said, knowing how to decode the chord symbols and melody alone doesn't mean someone can play the tune with others or improvise freely over it, since those are separate skills the sheet assumes of the user. Kress <Cite n={2} title="Multimodality" /> argues that a curriculum built on one linear mode of representation can "entrench inequality" and privilege learners who already have access to that mode over those who don't.

**What was carried across in the translation, and what was lost?** Transforming this into a game meant leaving behind the melody in favor of the tune's underlying harmony and the AABA format holding the song together. I also chose to highlight the iterative, mentor-guided learning of following, imitating, and improvising that characterizes an oral tradition. While I used AI throughout this process to help put together my artifact, I had to navigate its own register of authority and foreclosures rather than treat it as a neutral tool, especially around tempo versus time and the gestural cues I tried to embody in the game. What I ended up with reminds me of what Christine Sun Kim described in her TED talk: sight and sound functioning simultaneously, like a chord, rather than sequentially, the way notes on a page might be read one at a time <Cite n={5} title="The enchanting music of sign language" />.

**What does this reveal about the bodyminds the curriculum was designed for, and those it was not?** My transfiguration of Cotton Tail reveals that the original curriculum assumed a bodymind capable of solitary, page-based decoding, disadvantaging those who learn relationally and through other multimodal means <Cite n={1} title="DisCrit Solidarity" />. In a musical tradition built on listening, imitation, creation, and improvisation, a print modality can't capture the rich history and performative depth possible beyond the page. Moving this artifact away from a modality of isolated, individual mastery brings out the holistic spirit of performative arts, where bodyminds rely on each other's gestural cues and interdependence in real time.
