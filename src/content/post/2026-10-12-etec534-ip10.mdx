In [IP#2](/posts/2026-09-26-etec534-ip2/) I described white-out twice, once through McLuhan's tetrad and once through Latour, where the bottle was a delegate that we hand the hiding to and that hands "shake me, one thin coat, now wait" back to us. Going back to it now, both descriptions start with separate parties (a student, a mistake, a bottle) who then act on each other.

<Ip10Repaint />

New materialism starts before that. Toohey (2018) describes it as seeing "people, discourses, practices and things continually in relation, under construction and changing together" (p. 29), and Hill (2017) separates interaction, which "assumes distinct, independent entities", from intra-action, the "mutual constitution of entangled agencies" (p. 3). The mistake is then not sitting there before the bottle arrives. A wrong letter in pencil gets rubbed out and one on a screen gets deleted, so it only becomes something to cover where ink that will not lift meets a page that is supposed to be clean and a reader who marks it. The first thing I would change is my line that "what we delegated was the hiding", since the one who hides, the thing hidden and the one it is hidden from all get made in that arrangement.

The second change is to what I called prescription. Charteris et al. (2017) quote Shaviro on tools that "still have independent lives of their own", so that "we cannot just use them. We must learn to work with them" (p. 6). I read the wait for a patch to dry as an instruction handed back to us, and I would now describe it as the fluid's own tempo, where it thickens in the bottle, dries on the page, and cracks and flakes later whether anyone is watching or not. Bennett's (2010) point that nonhuman things "all have different types and degrees of power" (pp. 108–109) fits a small bottle well.

What I would add is where the solvent goes. In IP#2 I gave it a seat in Latour's assembly, for the adolescents who died sniffing it and for the ozone layer (King et al., 1985; Environment and Climate Change Canada, 2025). A new materialist account would follow the matter itself, since the evaporation that dries the patch is the same thing that was breathed in and that reached the ozone layer, and one of its solvents was phased out because of it. Charteris et al. describe classrooms as "assemblages of bodies, subjectivities, objects, ideas and discourses" (p. 7), and I suppose a pencil case is one too.

Hill (2017) changes the question I would ask in front of a patched page. In place of "what does it mean?" Hill borrows Lenz Taguchi's "how does it work?" (p. 4), and in a family quarrel moves the blame from the person to "the site of contact" (p. 13). For a student's patch that would mean asking what the pen, the paper, the fluid and my marking are producing together, before asking whose mistake is under it.
