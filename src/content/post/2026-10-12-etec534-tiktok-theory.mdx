## The feed I chose

What happens to a finding about human tutoring when it becomes a demonstration of an AI tutor? I followed that question through Sal Khan, Khan Academy's founder, whose promise of an AI tutor for every student is a public claim about how learning works, and whose video opens Watters's (2021) history of teaching machines (pp. 1–3). Since I couldn't find a verified account of his own, I used [@khanacademy](https://www.tiktok.com/@khanacademy), where he argues for AI tutoring in 2023. I looked closely at five clips that follow one claim to different audiences, and so my writing here speaks to [@khanacademy](https://www.tiktok.com/@khanacademy)'s feed rather than all of educational TikTok.

The text I chose to look at was [Bloom's (1984) two sigma paper](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X013006004), which was a study Khan names near the start of his TED talk (now turned TikTok) to support why a tutor for every student matters. Throughout this post, as I refer to educational theory, I mean something like Bloom's paper: an explanation of how learning works that someone could act on, along with the evidence and conditions the explanation hinges on. What I found in my explorations was that in these clips Bloom's finding keeps its promise but essentially loses what it rests on, and that who is speaking, what we get to see and who is being spoken to end up filling that gap.

## A text and its TikTok

Bloom (1984) reports that tutored students averaged about two standard deviations above students in conventional classes, in three-week studies where the tutoring came with formative tests and corrective feedback (pp. 4–5). His problem was how to get group instruction close to that at a cost most societies could bear (p. 4), so this is a finding under certain conditions with a question attached, and not a result for AI.

In [his TED talk](https://www.ted.com/talks/sal_khan_how_ai_could_save_not_destroy_education), Khan (2023) brings up Bloom's question and then restates it as "How do you actually give it to everyone in an economic way?" (1\:47), then answers it by calling for a tutor for every student.

Khan Academy's [53-second TikTok version of the TED talk](https://www.tiktok.com/@khanacademy/video/7228676240100642094) (Figure 1) rearranges three parts of this talk and puts it back together in a new order. It starts with the promise of a tutor for every student, stopping just before the sentence offering teachers an AI assistant. Then it moves to Khanmigo's features and demo, and it ends with Khan explaining Bloom: "It can take your below-average student and turn them into an above-average student." In the TED talk, "it" is one-to-one tutoring, while in the TikTok "it" means Khanmigo ("It can answer questions, it can quiz you"). This is to say that what started as Bloom's research question ends up looking more like a product advertisement than an educational finding.

## Who gets to explain it

Two days later [@khanacademy](https://www.tiktok.com/@khanacademy) reposted [a reaction video](https://www.tiktok.com/@khanacademy/video/7229392330904636714) by @westbytech, with the creator's watermark still on it (Figure 2). The creator says "I love Sal Khan" in front of the TED webpage, and the captions on screen go from "the 2 Sigma problem" to "the 2 Sigma opportunity", then to "the teacher happens to be the super intelligent [Khanmigo] AI". It has been played far more often than Khan Academy's own version of the talk, which shows how the research now has been filtered through someone else's excitement before the original account uses it to fold back into its own self-promotion.

## Talked about and talked to

Across these TikTok clips, students are the ones talked about, while the people talked to are adults: the public in [the Khanmigo launch](https://www.tiktok.com/@khanacademy/video/7210455960052124970), the TikTocked TED talk and the reaction, and teachers in [the testimonial](https://www.tiktok.com/@khanacademy/video/7506264754525097258). Parents get [the outright advertisement](https://www.tiktok.com/@khanacademy/video/7291047148844682542), which shows an adult and a child at a laptop with a caption offering "Kids + AI = Personalized learning" for "Just $9 a month!", so personalization becomes something a parent can now purchase.

The original word "tutor" drops out of the captions after 2023 completely, and by June 2024 Khanmigo is phrased as ["your AI-powered teaching assistant"](https://www.tiktok.com/@khanacademy/video/7377405244524875051). Going by who comments, the account's roughly 80,000 followers seem to include teachers, parents and adult learners, with the visible accounts it follows mostly being teachers and other education accounts, along with TED and a few miscellaneous accounts.

## Theory and practice

The TikTok teacher testimonial of Khanmigo is worth pointing out in regards to its approach and demonstration of the tool. It is filmed selfie-style around a classroom and never shows the tool itself in action (Figure 4), instead choosing to show evidence through a teacher's word and a classroom. One of its selling points is that students' basic questions "are already addressed, which allows me the opportunity to meet one on one with students or in small groups". There is a comparison to be made here between Watters (2021) who describes Sidney Pressey promising in the 1920s that machines would free a teacher for their "real function" (p. 9), and what TikTok brings to this concept is who makes that promise and in what way (now with a teacher talking to other teachers about a machine for teaching).

Taking this to my own classroom, I would wonder whether that time is still there once I have checked the tool's feedback, how much of it reaches small groups, and whose difficulties go unnoticed. Bloom's (1984) tutors followed feedback with corrective work and a parallel test (p. 4), so faster feedback on its own doesn't explain much unless students do something with it, which becomes the direction I would check for learning rather than counting the tool use itself as learning.

## What happens to the theory

Through this process, TikTok gave Bloom's theory a face and a demonstration of how others could carry it into new conversations and contexts while the theory's evidence simultaneously gets left behind. This seems to be a choice more so than something TikTok forces, though, since these clips seek out new users and promotional sign-ups. Watters (2021) argues that the media's "ed-tech imaginary" has "in many ways, been more influential than the history or science of teaching and learning" (pp. 10–11), and this post's findings add to that sentiment. The theory that travels most easily here is that personalization can be delivered through a tool, leaving teachers to work out themselves what actually changes in practice.
