## The feed I chose

For this task I followed Sal Khan through Khan Academy's TikTok feed ([@khanacademy](https://www.tiktok.com/@khanacademy)), as no verified account under his own name turned up. He is not an education scholar, but his promise that artificial intelligence can give every student a personal tutor is a claim about how learning works. Watters (2021) opens *Teaching Machines* with another of his videos, an eleven-minute history of education that "is all wrong" (p. 3). As of October 12, 2026 the account had about 79,500 followers, followed 139 accounts and had posted 199 videos since July 2022. This analysis is based on 18 of them, the ten most recent (August to October 2026) and eight older ones (2023 to 2025) found through search results and the Internet Archive.

## What it talks about, and to whom

In the spring of 2023 the feed is Khan himself. One clip is [his announcement of Khanmigo](https://www.tiktok.com/@khanacademy/video/7210455960052124970), a horizontal video with his name and title on it set inside the vertical frame, where he says that "artificial intelligence needs to be a tool for real learning and not for cheating" and that the technology could "empower the lives of billions". Another is cut from his TED talk. After that he is gone from my sample (he speaks in 2 of the 18 clips), and the people the feed is speaking to keep changing.

## A text and its TikTok

The text I compared the feed to is the transcript of that TED talk (Khan, 2023), which runs 15 minutes and 36 seconds. Near the start Khan shows a figure from "Benjamin Bloom's 1984 2 sigma study" and explains that one-to-one tutoring moved students two standard deviations up the bell curve, and then says, "Just to put that in plain language, that could take your average student and turn them into an exceptional student." He adds that Bloom "framed it as a problem" because there was no economic way to give a tutor to everyone, and a little later he points out a safeguard, that the chatbot's conversation "is recorded and viewable by your teacher".

[The TikTok made from this talk](https://www.tiktok.com/@khanacademy/video/7228676240100642094) is 53 seconds long and is spliced from three places. It opens with the promise from 0:21 ("giving every student on the planet an artificially intelligent but amazing personal tutor"), jumps to the list of what Khanmigo can do from 4:21, and ends on the plain-language line from 1:36. Bloom, the bell curve, the word "problem" and the promise of an assistant for teachers are all gone, and the safeguard survives only as small print on the screen. In the talk, "that could take your average student" points back at human tutoring in Bloom's study. In the TikTok the same sentence follows the description of the chatbot, so it now reads as something Khanmigo does.

Two days later the account reposted [a video by a tech creator](https://www.tiktok.com/@khanacademy/video/7229392330904636714) reacting to the same talk. The creator stands in front of the TED page and says "I love Sal Khan", and the captions on the video read "which he talks about the 2 Sigma problem" and "now he calls it the 2 Sigma opportunity", a phrase Khan uses near the end of the talk. That clip has been played about 291,000 times, against about 21,000 for Khan Academy's own cut.

## What TikTok gives a theory, and what it takes away

If educational theory is a claim about how learning works that someone could act on, and that says what it rests on, then the version of Bloom that Khan gives on the TED stage has a finding, a method (one tutor, one student) and a limit (cost). What survives the cut is the finding with its source taken off. On TikTok the claim gets a face and a demo on the screen, and it can travel as somebody else's enthusiasm, which in this case went about fourteen times further than the account's own cut. I suppose the green screen is the platform's kind of citation, since the creator has the TED page behind them and tells viewers to go and watch the whole talk, while the account's own clips only point to the link in the bio.

Watters (2021) writes that the "ed-tech imaginary" shaped by "newspapers, magazines, television, and film" has "in many ways, been more influential than the history or science of teaching and learning" (pp. 10–11), and I would add this feed to that list. The book's conclusion describes new teaching machines that "claim to be able to respond more rapidly, more intelligently, more efficiently than a human teacher or even a human tutor could" (pp. 254–255), which is close to what the 53-second cut says about Khanmigo.

## Theory and practice

The clip closest to my own work is the one made for teachers. The teacher's last reason is that students' basic questions get answered by the tool, "which allows me the opportunity to meet one on one with students or in small groups". Watters (2021) quotes the same promise from Sidney Pressey in the 1920s, that a machine would free a teacher for the "real function" of "inspirational and thought-stimulating activities", individual attention included (p. 9). The theory of the machine is the same a hundred years apart, and what TikTok changes is who says it, since it is now a teacher talking to other teachers and not the inventor or the founder. The practice question the clip never gets to is whether that one-on-one time happens, which a teacher would have to find out in their own room.

## What I could not see

The task suggests looking for a change around the 2024 US election. My 18 clips have a gap between February 2024 and May 2025, so I cannot say what the feed did that fall. None of the 18 mentions an election, a party or a policy, and the nearest thing to a public argument is in Khan's two clips from 2023. I also did not get to look through the 139 accounts it follows.

Going by this one feed, what stays on TikTok is the promise and the person making it, while the study it rests on does not come along. The full talk has about 4.3 million views on TED's own site, far more than either clip, so TikTok is not where most people met the idea, though teachers are now among the people these clips are made for.
