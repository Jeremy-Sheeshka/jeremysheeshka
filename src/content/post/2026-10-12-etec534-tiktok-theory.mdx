## The feed I chose

What happens to a finding about human tutoring when it becomes a demonstration of an AI tutor? I followed that question through Sal Khan, Khan Academy's founder, whose promise of an AI tutor for every student is a public claim about how learning works, and whose video opens Watters's (2021) history of teaching machines (pp. 1–3). As no verified account of his own turned up, I used [@khanacademy](https://www.tiktok.com/@khanacademy), where he speaks only in the 2023 clips I found. I looked closely at five clips chosen to follow one claim across audiences, so they cannot stand for educational TikTok as a whole.

My text is [Bloom's (1984) two sigma paper](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X013006004), with Khan's (2023) TED transcript and Watters's _Teaching Machines_. By educational theory I mean an explanation of how learning works that someone could act on, along with what it rests on, and by remediation its reworking in another medium, which changes its authority and what it asks of people. What I found is that in these clips the finding keeps its promise but loses what it rests on, and who speaks and what we see fill the gap.

## A text and its TikTok

Bloom (1984) reports tutored students averaging about two standard deviations above conventionally taught ones in three-week studies in grades four, five and eight, where the tutoring came with formative tests and corrective feedback (pp. 4–5). His problem was bringing group instruction close to that at a cost schools could bear, so this is a finding under conditions, with a question attached, not a result for AI.

In [his TED talk](https://www.ted.com/talks/sal_khan_how_ai_could_save_not_destroy_education), Khan (2023) restates the problem as "How do you actually give it to everyone in an economic way?" (1\:47) and answers with a tutor for every student, where Bloom had looked for better group teaching. That is a reasonable hypothesis, since the talk shows Khanmigo diagnosing a student's error, though TED had already staged it with a product demo.

[The 53-second TikTok cut](https://www.tiktok.com/@khanacademy/video/7228676240100642094) (Figure 1) splices three parts of the talk: the tutor promise (0\:21), ending just before the sentence offering teachers an AI assistant; Khanmigo's features and the "Why do I need to learn this?" demo (4\:21); and Khan's gloss of Bloom (1\:36): "That could take your average student and turn them into an exceptional student. It can take your below-average student and turn them into an above-average student." In the talk, "it" is one-to-one tutoring. In the TikTok, "it" has meant Khanmigo for half a minute ("It can answer questions, it can quiz you"), and the cut back to Khan comes while the chatbot is still typing, so a research question becomes something a product seems ready to answer.

## Who gets to explain it

Two days later the account reposted [a reaction](https://www.tiktok.com/@khanacademy/video/7229392330904636714) by @westbytech, keeping the creator's watermark (Figure 3). The creator says "I love Sal Khan" in front of the TED webpage, and the on-screen captions move from "the 2 Sigma problem" to "the 2 Sigma opportunity", then to "the teacher happens to be the super intelligent [Khanmigo] AI". Played about 291,400 times, fourteen times the account's own cut, the research now travels as someone else's enthusiasm, folded back into Khan Academy's promotion.

I suppose the webpage is the platform's kind of citation, though it points to TED rather than Bloom's methods, which his article keeps inside the argument. Neither form guarantees a sound claim, but they make different checks easy.

## Talked about and talked to

Across the five clips, students are talked about while adults are talked to: the public in the launch, the TED cut and the reaction, and teachers in [the testimonial](https://www.tiktok.com/@khanacademy/video/7506264754525097258) ("ELA teachers, I have a new resource for you"). Parents get [the advertisement](https://www.tiktok.com/@khanacademy/video/7291047148844682542), which shows an adult and child at a laptop turned away from us, asks how children can "navigate safely" and ends "Meet me in the caption", where "Kids + AI = Personalized learning" costs "Just $9 a month!" (the theory at its shortest, with a price). The only thing in these clips that speaks to a student is the chatbot, whose window in [the launch](https://www.tiktok.com/@khanacademy/video/7210455960052124970) warns that the chat is "viewable by your parent and teacher".

On November 2, 2026 the profile showed about 79,900 followers and 139 followed accounts, though not who they are. Replies were possible, yet the TED cut drew 12 comments and the advertisement none.

## Theory and practice

The teacher testimonial is closest to my own classroom concerns. Filmed selfie-style, jumping around a classroom with "feedback" and "checker" enlarged in the captions, it never shows the tool (Figure 4), so the evidence is the teacher's word and room. Its last selling point is that students' basic questions "are already addressed, which allows me the opportunity to meet one on one with students or in small groups", which turns the TED cut around, since the AI that was the tutor there now frees the teacher to tutor. Watters (2021) describes Sidney Pressey promising in the 1920s that machines would free a teacher for her "real function" (p. 9), and what TikTok changes is who makes that promise, a teacher talking to other teachers.

In my own classroom, I would wonder whether that time survives checking the feedback, how much reaches small groups, and whose difficulties go unnoticed. Bloom's (1984) tutors followed feedback with corrective work and a parallel test (p. 4), so faster feedback explains little unless students use it, which is what I would check rather than counting tool use as learning.

## What happens to the theory

In this feed, TikTok gives the theory a face and a demonstration, and lets one account tell the public the AI is the tutor while telling teachers it frees them to tutor. What gets left behind is what the theory rests on, though that is a choice, since these clips spend their one link on sign-ups. Far more people met the idea through TED (4.4 million views, against the cut's 21,300 plays), but TikTok is where parents and teachers are addressed directly. Watters (2021) argues the media's "ed-tech imaginary" has "in many ways, been more influential than the history or science of teaching and learning" (pp. 10–11), and this feed adds to it. The theory that travels most easily here is that personalization can be delivered through a tool, leaving teachers to establish what it changes in practice.
