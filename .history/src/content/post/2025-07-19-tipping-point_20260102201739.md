<div class="dropdown-item">
  <div class="dropdown-question">Introduction</div>
  <div class="dropdown-answer">
    <p style="color: var(--theme-text) !important;"> One of our projects for ETEC 511: New Foundations of Educational Technology was to identify a significant shift and technological change in an educational technology where the adoption of a newer technology displaces a previous one. <br> <br>
    For this group project, we have chosen to focus on the small-scale tipping point case of the technology adoption of Mathology through the lenses of: differentiated learning, differentiated instruction, and differentiated assessment.
    </p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="dropdown-item">
  <div class="dropdown-question">What is Mathology?</div>
  <div class="dropdown-answer">
    <p style="color: var(--theme-text) !important;">Mathology is a digital and print math resource that shifts learning from traditional paper-and-pencil tasks to more hands-on, engaging, and accessible learning experiences. It includes interactive activities, sample unit plans, “little books”, and built-in assessments that support all learners. Mathology supports differentiated learning by meeting students where they’re at in terms of current proficiency. Depending on the individual student, Mathology can assist them with extra support, extension, or something in between. This digital platform blends core instructional strategies with tools that allow for differentiated instruction and assessment in real-time. Inspired by the idea of “Mathland,” where students explore and build stronger foundations of math in authentic, meaningful, and situated ways, Mathology helps create inclusive, accessible, student-centered classrooms where every learner can thrive. 
    </p>
    <img src="/assets/images/blog/mathologylogo.png" alt="Mathology Logo" style="display:block; margin: 10px auto; max-width: 200px;" />
    <p align="center" style="color: var(--theme-text) !important;"><small>etr.mathology.ca/</small></p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="dropdown-item">
  <div class="dropdown-question">What systems or tools does it displace?</div>
  <div class="dropdown-answer">
    <p style="color: var(--theme-text) !important;">
    Mathology represents a cross-curricular shift in the elementary math classroom by displacing traditional teaching tools and systems with digital and printed counterparts for educators. Where teachers often rely on calculators and counting manipulatives in routines and curriculum, Mathology’s digital offerings package this workflow of lesson preparation, delivery, and assessment together in an interactive way that blends different areas of learning, literacies, and content knowledge. Whether it be dice, playing cards, cubes, blocks, worksheets, or even textbooks, Mathology represents a shift in physical artifacts of math teaching that focuses on an accessible learner- centered experience.  <br><br>
    </p>
    <img src="/assets/images/blog/tpticeberg.png" alt="Tipping Point Iceberg" />
    <p align="center" style="color: var(--theme-text) !important;"><a href="/assets/images/blog/tpticeberg.png" target="_blank" style="color: #3182ce !important;"> Click for Full Size </a></p>
    <p style="color: var(--theme-text) !important;">By bundling together differentiated instructional materials, Mathology transcends analog resource management, shakes up past pedagogical routines and practices, and displaces paper-and-pencil assessments through a single multi-modal platform.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="dropdown-item">
  <div class="dropdown-question">Usability of Mathology</div>
  <div class="dropdown-answer">
    <p style="color: var(--theme-text) !important;"><strong>Working with Woolgar</strong><br><br>
    One aspect of Mathology in particular worth noting is in how the tool takes into consideration the user’s experience, attention, and usability of the tool itself on a digital level.<br><br>
    The streamline process of which the content is distributed and accessed by students (what the user can and cannot do), along with user feedback cycles, are both factors which influence the platforms overall usability and actively shape how students interact with mathematics. 
    <br><br>
    Where previously students worked with objects or printed worksheets for math at an individual pace, Mathology offers clickable manipulatives, and real-time information about the students learning on a dashboard. The instantaneous feedback, along with the prescribed sequences and actions of how students are able to interact with the on-screen material (dragging, clicking, uncovering etc.), inside of a story-driven environment, are all design choices that effectively make Mathology rise above the usability of traditional tools, systems and technologies that came before it.
    </p>
    <img src="/assets/images/blog/mathologyintroduction.png" alt="Mathology Introduction" />
    <p align="center" style="color: var(--theme-text) !important;"><a href="/assets/images/blog/mathologyintroduction.png" target="_blank" style="color: #3182ce !important;"> Click for Full Size </a></p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="dropdown-item">
  <div class="dropdown-question">The Tipping Point of Mathematical Modelling</div>
  <div class="dropdown-answer">
    <p style="color: var(--theme-text) !important;"><strong>Moving from In-Person to Online with Mathology</strong><br><br>
    The COVID-19 pandemic marked a critical tipping point for digital educational resources. School systems across the globe shifted from in-person to online learning. The transition of mathematical modelling moved from concrete materials in the classroom to online platforms with digital resources. Pearson Canada Mathology launched their first prototype testing in 2019 which became a central resource for many educators during the pandemic in 2020. The pandemic created both a necessity and opportunity for its digital features that were accessible, user-friendly, and aligned with curriculum goals. “Little books” that were available in print, for instance, were accessible for children and teachers online. Mathematical manipulatives such as linking cubes, number lines, and coloured counters were used in the classroom to concretely model mathematical thinking in elementary classrooms. This required both students and educators to transition from traditional, concrete instructional methods to more abstract approaches. 
    </p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="dropdown-item">
  <div class="dropdown-question">Examining the Digital Divide</div>
  <div class="dropdown-answer">
    <p style="color: var(--theme-text) !important;">
    While some classrooms transitioned smoothly, others faced significant obstacles. Unfortunately, the shift highlighted digital inequities deeply rooted in socioeconomic status and geographic location. Sievertsen and Burgess (2020) argued that “school closures will increase inequality in learning,” (Sievertsen et. al, 2020). These gaps reflect global health inequalities where those with fewer resources were most at risk.<br><br>
    Students who did not have consistent and reliable internet access, working devices, or adult support at home faced challenges with engaging or even accessing Mathology’s online features. We might begin to analyze and critique the ways in which the digital education gap mirrors global health inequities. Access to essential services such as education or healthcare is influenced by systemic factors like income and infrastructure.
    </p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="dropdown-item">
  <div class="dropdown-question">Digital Labour & Sustainability</div>
  <div class="dropdown-answer">
    <p style="color: var(--theme-text) !important;">With the tipping point case of Mathology, the issues of digital labour and sustainability are intertwined. Teachers have a more efficient and pedagogically effective assignment and assessment system that saves them time. <br><br>
    From a sustainability perspective, Mathology's long-term potential can be examined from three key dimensions:
    </p>

<div class="dropdown-item">
  <div class="dropdown-question">Conclusion</div>
  <div class="dropdown-answer">
    <p style="color: var(--theme-text) !important;"><strong>Innovation and Inequity</strong><br><br>
    Mathology re-imagines math learning through hands-on digital interactivity, immediate feedback, and a storytelling framework that challenges the status quo of traditional teaching tools. It exemplifies media convergence and rethinks the cost of hidden digital labour requirements of educators within the digital platform.
    <br><br>   
    Ultimately, what defines a tipping point is not just the arrival of something new, but the value it carries for the users, the labour it demands, and the possibilities it opens or displaces for educators and learners alike.
