Media Ecology · Week 1 · A Visual Summary in Three Probes

The Empty Room Is Not Empty

A triptych cinemagraph of one classroom, read through Strate & Lum’s ecology of technics and de Castell, Droumeva & Jenson’s “building as interface.” The still frame is the environment you cannot see; the single looping trace in each panel is the probe that wakes you to it.

Form of the piece = its argument. A cinemagraph freezes everything but one motion; that asymmetry is McLuhan’s problem and remedy. The room is photographed empty on purpose: with no bodies in it, the roles the building “assigns to us and insists on our playing them” stand exposed as pure form (Postman 1970, 162, qtd in de Castell et al. 2014, 77).

Motion note (disclosure, because media ecology cares about the conditions of its own making): the traces above are synthetic light-probes rendered in CSS on a single still, not recorded footage. They are deliberately abstract — a sign of the invisible, the way a cinemagraph’s loop is a trace rather than a story. To make them empirical instead, lock a tripod on a real room and capture a genuine draft, a real fluorescent flicker, a nudged chair, then mask everything but that region (see the [SWAP] comments). Either way the logic is identical: freeze the environment, move one element, watch the water appear.

1. How media ecology “asks the right questions”

Strate and Lum’s media ecology is less a fixed theory than an orientation: the study of media as environments, and — reciprocally, and this is the move that matters for a classroom — of environments as media (Lum 2000, 1–2; de Castell et al. 2014, 77). Its founding conviction, running from Geddes through Mumford, Innis, McLuhan and Postman, is that the form of a technology or symbolic system, not merely its content, organizes cognition, social relations, and the very sense of what is real, largely below the threshold of conscious notice (Lum 2000, 2).

That is why the field refuses the question that dominates the rest of communication research. Against the “administrative school” with its roots in behaviourist, short-range effects studies (Gerbner, qtd in Lum 2000, 2–3), media ecology does not ask does medium X cause effect Y? It asks instead: what kind of world does this medium presuppose and cultivate? What does it make visible or invisible? What does it discipline? Whose time and attention does it organize? What persists beneath the surface change? The form of the inquiry is encoded in Nystrom’s generalizations, which Lum reproduces as the discipline’s compass: because media differ in symbolic form (the code, e.g. analogic vs. digital; prepositional vs. presentational) they carry different intellectual and emotional biases; because they differ in physical form (the technology that carries, stores, transmits the code) they carry different temporal, spatial and sensory biases; and from these follow political, social, metaphysical, content and finally epistemological biases (Nystrom, qtd in Lum 2000, 2). Bias-of-form, in other words, is the unit of analysis — never the isolated effect.

“Media ecology is the Toronto School, and the New York School. It is technological determinism, hard and soft… It is grammar and rhetoric, semiotics and systems theory… Media ecology is all of these things, and quite a bit more.” — Lance Strate, 1999, qtd in Lum 2000, 1

Strate’s deliberately capacious definition is not vagueness; it is a methodological stance. It signals that the field is constitutionally interdisciplinary and will not collapse into one dogma or one instrument. And the field’s own status is left unresolved on purpose. Postman’s question — is this a science, a branch of philosophy, or a form of social criticism? “Where, in short, do we place it in the catalogue?” (Postman 1988, qtd in Lum 2000, 2) — is not a weakness to be repaired but a discipline to be kept: a refusal of the administrative impulse to settle a question before it has been properly asked. Media ecology asks the right questions precisely because it will not close them prematurely. Its intellectual family shares, above all, the conviction that technics and culture co-evolve rather than one simply causing the other (Strate & Lum 2000, 60; Kuhns 1971, qtd therein).

Mumford is the keystone, and he is the reason a building can be a medium at all. Nystrom’s 1973 dissertation names Technics and Civilization (1934) as the field’s founding work (Strate & Lum 2000, 56), and Mumford as the first of the New York School. Three of his ideas do the heavy lifting here. His epochal historiography divides history not by dynasties but by the energy–matter complex of its technics — eotechnic (water-and-wood), paleotechnic (coal-and-iron), neotechnic (electricity-and-alloy) — each phase, in his words, “bring[ing] into existence particular types of workers, train[ing] them in particular ways, develop[ing] certain aptitudes and discourag[ing] others” (Mumford 1934, 109–110, qtd in Strate & Lum 2000, 58–59). His techno-organicism insists technology and biology are continuous, not opposed: “there was nothing uniquely human in tool-making until it was modified by linguistic symbols, esthetic designs, and socially transmitted knowledge” — what makes us human is language, art and play, not labour (Mumford 1967, 5, qtd in Strate & Lum 2000, 62–63). And his critique of the megamachine delivers the insight that reframes the entire photograph: the first machine was not made of iron but of human bodies, assembled by a “single mind… at the head of the organization,” disciplined by writing (whose earliest use was not ideas but temple records of grain and cattle — accountability) and, decisively, by time — the monastery bell and then the mechanical clock, which “is not merely a means of keeping track of the hours, but of synchronizing the actions of men” (Mumford 1967, 191–192; 1934, 13–14, qtd in Strate & Lum 2000, 67–71). The empty classroom in Panel II is that clock, still ticking, with the workers removed.

2. What an educational media ecology must involve

If the school is itself a medium — and Postman, recalling his undergraduate days, held exactly this: “the school was just one medium through which a culture communicated some of its important ideas” (Postman, qtd in Lum 2000, 2) — then an educational media ecology cannot stop at the laptops and learning-management systems inside the room. Following de Castell, Droumeva and Jenson’s “Building as Interface,” it must treat the school itself as a medium with real biases. A building “structures what we can see and say and, therefore, do… it assigns roles to us and insists on our playing them; it specifies what we are permitted to do and what we are not” (Postman 1970, 162, qtd in de Castell et al. 2014, 77). Concretely, such an ecology has to encompass the following specific elements, which fall into three families — physical, institutional, practical — mapped onto the three panels above.

Physical · the vessel

Architecture and material infrastructure as an energy–matter complex: ceiling tiles and their water-stains, fluorescent light and its flicker, the radiator/HVAC, window orientation and the dusk beyond, acoustics, air quality, mold, asbestos, leaks, draft, dust — the “aversive and dysfunctional interface” that does not merely house learning but forecloses it (de Castell et al. 2014, 78–79). Plus furniture and layout: the rows, the collaborative kidney table, the manipulatives in their tubs, the labelled “Science Center,” the alphabet rug as a literal grid imposed on the floor, the door as threshold, even the guitar pinned to the wall.

Institutional · the clock

Time and its discipline — bells, semesters, credit hours, the period block — as direct descendants of Mumford’s monastic and mechanical clock, turning pupils into interchangeable human parts of the megamachine (Strate & Lum 2000, 67–71). The hidden curriculum and the regime of pinned visibility (what gets displayed is what counts); the partitioning of knowledge into “centers”; the front-facing desk that places a single “mind at the head of the organization”; and the political economy of who funds, owns and builds the tools of learning versus who is merely permitted to consume them.

Practical · the body

The gap between objective and felt conditions — the SEE project found that measured sound did not predict experienced distraction, so environment is experiential and cultural, not only physical (de Castell et al. 2014, 82). The social life of hallways; sense of place or “home”; student satisfaction; play and art as the organic the machine cannot digest (Mumford’s techno-organicism); place as itself pedagogical, the Reggio “third teacher” (Gandini 1998, qtd in de Castell et al. 2014, 80); and sustainability in its fullest sense — financial and institutional, but also literally ecological.

Read together, these are not a checklist but a convergent method — the same interdisciplinary, multi-sensory stacking (light readings + sound readings + photography + narrative “talk-bubbles”) that let de Castell’s team see a building as an interface rather than a container (de Castell et al. 2014, 80–82). No single panel “is” media ecology; the ecology is the relation among the three, which is exactly what Strate’s capacious definition demands.

The “right questions” this theory lets us ask

  • What does a building teach apart from its official curriculum? (Panel I) The stained ceiling and the failing plant are not background; they are a lesson in neglect, a material argument about whose comfort the institution values.
  • What forms of attention or docility does a schedule of bells and blocks produce, regardless of content? (Panel II) The metronomic light asks you to feel time-as-regulation in your body, the way the canonical hours once did.
  • Who is permitted to build the tools of learning, and who is only permitted to use them? (Panel III) The door and the glowing rug ask who stands at the threshold of production versus mere consumption.
  • Can new pedagogies take root inside containers engineered for an obsolete, industrial-era conception of the student — or must the container itself become an interface, something built and rebuilt rather than simply inhabited? (all three)

3. What education has been, is, and should become

To ask those questions honestly is to take a position on education itself. Has been: schooling, in Mumford’s terms, is the paleotechnic inheritance of the megamachine — “seat-warming” that buys years of credentialing, the era of “sitting day after day in one after another small classroom with one teacher and the same group of students, year after year,” now “as obsolete as its coercive attentional economy” (de Castell et al. 2014, 88; cf. Mumford 1934, 14). Is: a contested present in which we eagerly embrace “21st-century learning” even as “prospects for a 22nd century are so rapidly receding from view” (de Castell et al. 2014, 75) — the dusk in Panel I, the outside going dark while the fluorescent clock keeps time indoors. Should become: not a better-consumed product but a built one.

De Castell’s turn from building-as-noun to building-as-verb — Fuller’s “I seem to be a verb,” Franklin’s contrast between technology’s artifactual and its social-practice sense (de Castell et al. 2014, 83–84) — is the ecological ethics of Mumford translated into pedagogy. A production pedagogy refuses the distribution of the sensible that consigns teachers and students to consumers of purchased capacity, the way text-based “specialized” tools once rendered teachers consumers where they had been producers (de Castell et al. 2014, 84–85). It insists, with Kress and the New London Group’s “critical making,” that we need not critical consumers but critical designers; that “in a process of enlightenment, there can only be participants” (Habermas 1975, 40, qtd in de Castell et al. 2014, 87); and that a system which produces its own future capacity is sustainable in a way a system that buys that capacity never is. That is Mumford’s hope — the passage “from power to plenitude,” megatechnics replaced by biotechnics, the machine brought back under human and organic harmony (Mumford 1970, 395, qtd in Strate & Lum 2000, 76–77) — arrived at in a classroom.

So the empty room in these three panels is not a void. It is a blueprint with the workers removed, waiting. The probes move through it the way a counter-environment moves through a sleeper: not to depict the child, the bell, or the draft, but to make you see the water. The container becomes an interface only when its inhabitants look up from inhabiting it and begin, together, to rebuild it. That is the right question media ecology lets us ask — and, as de Castell’s closing line insists, the only answer that is “no education at all” if we do not live it (de Castell et al. 2014, 87).
