Curriculum Otherwise · a reimagined sight-reading studio

Rhythm literacy built with the room, not around it.

Praxis began as a standard rhythm drill: a fixed canon, a metronome, a red mark for a wrong answer. This studio reimagines that premise as a universal, constructivist environment — one that assumes every student already arrives with rhythm, and asks the platform to notice, hold, and build from what they bring in.

What changed, and why

Three design moves reshape the original tool. Look for the small RESIST, RELATE, and IMAGINE tags throughout the studio — open any of them, or use the tabs on the right edge of the screen, to read the annotation attached to that decision.

How to move through it

Start in the Dashboard to choose a rhythm collection. From there, practice alone in Free Play, or negotiate a shared phrase with a partner in Trade Fours. When you're ready, visit the Gallery for the literature behind this redesign and one prompt for trying it yourself.

Gallery · literature and an invitation

Read what shaped this, then try it yourself.

This studio was built in conversation with a handful of ideas about datasets, feedback, and who a "universal" exercise is designed for. The reading below informed specific decisions inside the app; the prompt at the end is for you.

Literature consulted
  • Alvarez, M. (2019). (Digital) media as critical pedagogy. Media Theory, 3(1), 73–102. Behind the move from a passive drill to an active, co-constructed studio — resisting the "learned passivity" of teaching machines.
  • Bossavit, B., & Parsons, S. (2018). Outcomes for design and learning when teenagers with autism codesign a serious game. Journal of Computer Assisted Learning, 34, 293–305. Behind designing with, not merely for, the learners the tool claims to serve.
  • British Columbia Ministry of Education and Child Care. (n.d.). Arts education: Music, kindergarten to grade 9. Building Student Success – B.C. Curriculum. The mandate to explore identity, place, culture, and belonging through artistic experience.
  • D'Ignazio, C., & Klein, L. F. (2020). Data feminism. MIT Press. Behind naming Western classical notation as a "privilege hazard" — a default treated as neutral.
  • Gillespie, T. (2010). The politics of 'platforms'. New Media & Society, 12(3), 347–364. Behind treating the app as a platform whose design carries politics, not a neutral tool.
  • Graham, M. F. (2024). The effects of peer-assisted learning on rhythmic and melodic sight-reading in a middle school chorus. International Journal of Music Education. Behind Trade Fours — symmetrical peer-assisted learning over isolated reading.
  • Kalantzis, M., & Cope, B. (2010). The teacher as designer: Pedagogy in the new media age. E-Learning and Digital Media, 7(3), 200–222. Behind the dashboard as a design surface, and the teacher as designer of learning environments.
  • Ọnụọha, M. (2016). The library of missing datasets [Installation]. Behind the Cultural Datasets feature — the blank spots that reveal what a system deems worth collecting.
  • Reifinger, J. L., Jr. (2020). Teaching pitch notation–reading skills. General Music Today, 33(3), 21–28. Behind the enactive scaffold — building audiation through body and voice before the symbol.
  • Stenberg, A., & Cross, I. (2019). White spaces, music notation and the facilitation of sight-reading. Scientific Reports, 9, Article 5299. Behind the visual chunking of bars — reducing cognitive load as an inclusion issue.

A doing prompt for the cohort

Take a piece of music you're listening to right now and pull out a two-bar rhythm. Use the Cultural Datasets card to choose where it would live — Afro-Cuban, Aboriginal, or your own Local Family Database — then sketch, in a sentence or two, the developmental sequence you'd build from it.

Post your two bars and your sequence to the thread, and answer: What did the app assume about your rhythm that wasn't true — and what would have to change to fit what you brought in?
