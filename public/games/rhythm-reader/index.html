Curriculum Otherwise · a reimagined sight reading factory

Rhythm literacy centered around learners and communities.

Rhythm Reader is a free, in-browser tool for learning to read rhythm by sight, sound, and touch. Pick a counting aid, hear a pattern, tap it back, and grow at your own pace.

Step 4 - Attempt Sight Reading on Rhythm Reader›

How has the experience of sight reading these rhythms changed? Did you make use of any of the “accessibility options”? How helpful (or not) is the feedback? Is there anything else that might be missing?

Individual Responses
Chris Perry›

In Week 6, I looked at Sight Reading Factory through a purely defensive lens. As a jazz and ensemble player, I saw it as an automated gatekeeper that traded real bandstand listening for screen-based compliance, and because its Eurocentric design happened to fit my background, I fell right into the 'privilege hazard' D'Ignazio and Klein describe. But actually brainstorming alternative features with my group changed how I see the software. Discussing things like toggleable counting and subdivision scaffolding, offline worksheets, and a global rhythm bank showed me that critique is only the starting point. Designing forced us to stop just listing what's wrong with edtech and start asking what it takes to make a tool genuinely useful for students who don't fit the classical mold.

Samuel Teo›

While the critical reading revealed the limitations and affordances of Sight Reading Factory, it was only through the process of planning our redesigned artifact where I was able to examine the design choices that made using that tool difficult for me in the first place and how I could use them to improve accessibility, starting by removing the paywall. Combined with the course readings, I was able to implement collaboration by opening up our cultural “Rhythm Bank” which is designed in mind for users to contribute any new rhythms they find that may not already belong in the database. I imagine that there would be a panel of human musicians that verify the rhythms before they are added. I also wanted to let users be able to identify unknown rhythms as well – it is easy to look up a song by searching for lyrics or the artist, but very difficult to search for a rhythm using traditional means (e.g. Google), so I thought our artifact would be the perfect place to do it. This also expands our artifact and integrates it more with everyday life, functioning like a Shazam for rhythms when it’s not being used to practice sight reading. Our group also discussed how to provide alternative ways of breaking down the rhythms and the transparent feedback we wanted users to receive. We wanted the user interface to feel familiar but did not want our additional helpful options to be overwhelming, so they became toggleable and I made the suggestion of having them all be “off” by default. The act of redesigning let me envision how to enact accessible changes for our tool.

Jeremy Sheeshka›

In Week 6, I was still an outsider to SRF, having never had the budget to bring the technology into my classroom when I was teaching band. My curiosity about, and experiences with, SRF were still largely conceptual at that point. I had wondered whether the algorithm could generate material in the style of real composers, and how (if?) it represented place-based and cultural traditions entangled within various musical styles. Critically examining and engaging in a redesign of SRF brought to my attention the gaps and hidden curriculum it presented users with. Doing this design work made me acutely aware of the responsibility I had over how a user engaged with the app, and made it important for our group to build in solutions that increase accessibility for learners with different backgrounds and relationships to music.

Building in the toggleable modes during gameplay (numbers, takadimi, aural playback, draw, and my personal favourite: dinosaur mode), as well as imagining how the co-op mode would work, forced me to confront how enactive/symbolic distinctions were being made by the end-user and how to accommodate this through tools that assist with decoding rhythm, whether through notation or multimodal alternatives like a dinosaur runner game. That said, it is clear to me that clicking a mouse or tapping a spacebar does not fuel the same kinesthetic learning and building of muscle memory that clapping a rhythm in a circle or feeling it lock in with other players would.

Annotating our redesign through RESIST, RELATE, and IMAGINE helped me understand that there is no one-size-fits-all approach, and that a tool which meets someone where they are can accelerate learning without forcing everyone through the same path. In the case of our redesign, simply including an option to listen to a rhythm before trying to read and play it opened up new possibilities for user choice and flow. The Rhythm Bank extends this further by grounding rhythmic reading in place-based and cultural traditions, while offering an option to connect locally through a classroom or community of music learners. While something like this generative rhythm-reading sandbox could have helped me in my own practice, working through 16th note rhythms two bars at a time and reading with my ears rather than my eyes and brain, I only recognized what was missing once I was the one building the listen-first option.

What I have not figured out yet is whether the generated rhythms are musically sound and worth practicing. Inclusive scaffolding alone does not guarantee compositional quality, and my Week 6 question about whether that quality is there remains open. Even so, while naming SRF's limitations proved straightforward, building around them showed me that these were non-neutral choices made to cater to a particular audience of learners.
