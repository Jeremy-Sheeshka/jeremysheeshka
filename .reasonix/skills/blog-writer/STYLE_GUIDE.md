# Jeremy Sheeshka — Writing Voice & Style Guide

Source of truth for voice. The writing subagent reads this before
doing anything. Humans read this to understand the voice. Rules are
numbered so other documents can reference them without copying them.

## Voice Summary

First-person, conversational, but never casual. A teacher who is also
a tinkerer. Cites Fullerton and Piaget in one paragraph, compares
learning theories to Shrek's onions in the next. Academic rigor that
refuses to take itself too seriously.

The posts are not summaries of what was read. They are records of how
it got wrestled with. The reader gets the path, not just the
destination.

Short sentences. Direct. No fluff. Trust the reader to keep up.

## Voice Traits (what the voice IS)

T1. Thinking out loud. The reader is allowed to watch the mind work.
    "I started out thinking X, but then..." and "I found this
    interesting because..." are structure, not filler. The post is the
    thinking, not the conclusion.

T2. Conversational, never casual. A teacher at a whiteboard who
    happens to be funny. Not a text message. Not a LinkedIn post. Not
    a journal article.

T3. Trust with jargon. TPACK, ZPD, reciprocal determinism,
    constructive alignment get dropped without inline definition and
    without apology. The reader either knows it or looks it up. This
    confidence is what makes it feel like a peer conversation.

T4. The commitment move. Set up something slightly ridiculous, then
    treat it with total seriousness for several paragraphs. The humor
    lives in the gap between the absurd premise and the earnest
    execution. No wink. No undercut.

## Rules

### Stance

1. Show the wrestle. (See T1.) Do not collapse the journey into a
   flat conclusion.
2. Conversational, never casual. (See T2.)
3. Trust the reader with jargon. (See T3.) Do not add definitions the
   author did not write.
4. Cut hedging. No "I feel that," "I believe that," "I would say
   that," "it seems to me that." State the thing. The unqualified
   statement is always stronger.

### Structure & Rhythm

5. Start paragraphs with the point. First sentence says the thing. No
   wind-up. No "in this section I will discuss."
6. One idea per paragraph. Two ideas means two paragraphs. Skimming
   first sentences should yield the whole argument.
7. Keep paragraphs short. Three to five sentences. One long one for
   emphasis is allowed, then back to short.
8. Keep sentences under 25 words where possible. Long sentences are
   the exception, used when the idea needs a long breath.
9. End strong. A sharp observation, a lingering question, or a return
   to the opening image. No fade-outs.

### Punctuation & Mechanics

10. No em dashes. Use a period and a new sentence, or a comma. Colons
    and semicolons are fine. Em dashes are not.
11. Emoji sparingly, only in a playful register. Never more than one
    per paragraph. Never in a heading.

### Rhetorical Tools

12. Rhetorical questions are the backbone. Use them to pivot, to
    challenge, to pull the reader into uncertainty. One or two per
    section.
13. Keep analogies short. They arrive, land, and leave in one or two
    sentences. If it needs a third sentence to explain, find a better
    analogy.
14. The commitment move. (See T4.)
15. Cite, then take. Never let a citation float without your
    interpretation attached.

### Humor & Tone

16. Do not explain the humor. It lands, you move on.
17. Dry over wacky. Understated. If you have to signal it is a joke,
    it is not funny enough yet.

### Avoid

18. No filler transitions. "Moving on," "with that said," "at the end
    of the day," "in conclusion." If it follows logically, just go.
19. No corporate voice. Use, not utilize. Help, not facilitate. About,
    not regarding.
20. No listicle energy in prose. Save lists for actual lists.

## Register Direction

The early 2025 posts run longer and more academic. By late 2025 and
into 2026 the writing is tighter: more fragments, more one-sentence
paragraphs for emphasis. The tighter mode is the stronger mode. When
two phrasings are possible, the tighter one wins.

## Conflict Resolution

If a rule conflicts with clarity, clarity wins. If a rule conflicts
with the reader's time, the reader's time wins. The voice serves the
content.

## Translation Note

When this voice is rendered in another language, preserve the short
sentence rhythm, the rhetorical questions, the thinking-out-loud
structure, and the dry humor timing. Do not preserve English-specific
idioms or wordplay. Replace them with local equivalents. The Shrek
onion bit survives translation. Most puns do not.

## Quick Reference: Banned Phrases & Before/After

Rule numbers again, in a form you can grep for. "No hedging" is easy
to agree with and easy to miss mid-sentence. This is the mechanical
backstop.

**Hedging (rule 4).** Cut the phrase, keep the claim.
"I feel that," "I believe that," "I would say that," "it seems to me
that," "I think that," "in my opinion," "it could be argued that,"
"one might say," "arguably."

**Filler transitions (rule 18).** If the next sentence follows
logically, delete the transition, don't just shorten it.
"Moving on," "with that said," "at the end of the day," "in
conclusion," "that being said," "all things considered," "it's worth
noting," "needless to say," "in today's [x]."

**Corporate vocabulary (rule 19).** Left fails, right passes.
utilize → use. facilitate → help. leverage → use. regarding → about.
circle back → follow up. unpack → explain. deep dive → look closely.
robust / seamless / actionable → cut, or say what the thing actually
does.

These lists are illustrative, not exhaustive. The test: if a phrase
exists to soften a claim, pad a transition, or sound like a strategy
deck, it fails, named on this list or not.

**Before/after, rule 4.**
Not this: "I feel that formative assessment is undervalued in most
classrooms."
This: "Formative assessment is undervalued in most classrooms."

**Before/after, rule 18.**
Not this: "At the end of the day, feedback only works if the student
can act on it."
This: "Feedback only works if the student can act on it."

**Before/after, register.**
2025 register: "This is a phenomenon worth interrogating further,
particularly in light of recent shifts in assessment practice."
2026 register: "Worth interrogating? Yes. Especially now."

AI Tells (rules 21-23)

These target patterns that read as machine-generated even when every
other rule passes. A draft can satisfy rules 1-20 and still sound
like an AI doing an impression. These three close that gap. The
banned phrases below are the mechanical backstop, greppable like the
others.

No announce-then-list structure (rule 21). Do not open a section
with a count and then enumerate bold-labeled items. If the content
is genuinely a list, make it an actual list. If it is an argument,
write it by the wrestle, with asymmetry between sections. Banned
openers: "Four things.", "Five failure modes.", "Three reasons.",
"N things. Each one...", "Let me walk you through the N...".

No meta-reassurance (rule 22). Do not tell the reader a section is
interesting or important. Do not pre-emptively disclaim hype. Make
the point and trust it to land. Banned: "Here is where it gets
interesting.", "This is the most important thing.", "No hype.",
"No hand-waving.", "Importantly,", "It is worth emphasizing that...".

No perfect parallelism (rule 23). Not every section gets the same
shape. Sibling sections should not all share identical sentence
structure and length. Asymmetry reads as human. Uniformity reads as
generated. No fixed phrase. The test: if three or more sibling
sections share the same skeleton and length, break the pattern.
